The course in one story

The whole course answers one question: how can a computer get useful numbers out of text? It does this in three steps. Each step fixes the main weakness of the one before.

Step 1: Count the words. A computer can’t read, but it can count. So you tip each text’s words into a bag, like Scrabble tiles, and count them. A shop with 1,000 reviews gets a big table: one row per review, one column per word, the counts inside. The weakness: the order and the meaning are lost. “Dog bites man” and “man bites dog” give the same bag, and “bank” counts the same whether it is a river bank or a money bank.

Methods in this step (Block 2): cleaning the text first (removing very common words, cutting words to their root); adjusting the counts so texts compare fairly (shares, yes/no, and tf-idf, which gives rare words more weight); measuring how alike two texts are (cosine similarity); keyword searches and word lists (Boolean search, the EPU index, dictionary “tone” scores); predicting a number from the words (text regression with Lasso, a fine on big word weights); sorting texts into categories (Naive Bayes); finding themes by themselves (topic models: LSA and LDA).

Step 2: Give words a meaning, as a position on a map. Instead of counting words, give every word a spot on a “meaning map”, so similar words sit close together: “cheap” next to “inexpensive”, “cat” next to “dog”. The idea behind it: you can guess what a word means from the company it keeps, the way you’d guess an unknown word from the words around it. The weakness: each word gets one fixed spot, so “bank” still has one meaning in every sentence.

Methods in this step (Block 3): word-pair scores and squeezing a big table (the PMI table and SVD); word2vec, a small network that learns the map (and its “one-hot” input); comparing word arrows with cosine similarity.

Step 3: Read each word in its context. Modern language models (LLMs, like the one behind ChatGPT) read the whole sentence and give each word a meaning that fits that sentence. “Bank” next to “water” and “bank” next to “credit” get different positions. They also write, by guessing the next piece of text over and over.

Methods in this step (Block 4): next-word guessing, from the simplest counting models to LLMs (and the temperature dial that controls how bold the guesses are); tokens (the word pieces an LLM reads); the Transformer and its self-attention (each word looking at the other words); position information (so word order is not lost); how LLMs are trained; fine-tuning versus prompting; looking up documents before answering (RAG); and the problems LLMs cause (energy, copyright, made-up answers, privacy).

One thread through all three steps: who gives the answers? Supervised methods learn from examples with the right answer attached, or from a rule you fix yourself (so word lists count). Unsupervised methods get no answers and find patterns themselves (topic models). Self-supervised methods take the answers from the text itself, by hiding the next word and guessing it (word embeddings, LLMs). This sorting is a favourite True/False trap.

Three questions that pick the method for you:

  1. Do you already know what you’re looking for? Yes → Boolean search or a dictionary. No → a topic model.
  2. Do you have texts with answers attached (star ratings, tick-box answers, a sample you labelled by hand)? Yes → you can train a sorter, and you can check every other method against those answers.
  3. Does context matter (“not good”, sarcasm, words with two meanings)? Yes → a language model (fine-tuned or prompted), not word counting.

Part 1: Turning text into numbers (exam Block 2)

1. Cleaning text before analysis: the workflow, stop words, stemming vs lemmatisation, n-grams

On the exam: not its own question in 2025. Likely as True/False (“not” as a stop word, stemming vs lemmatisation, n-grams, which step a tool belongs to). Useful for the reflection (2025 Block 5, 20 points): cleaning choices and the four challenges are strong pitfalls to discuss.

The point of it

Cleaning is like peeling vegetables before cooking. It makes the same word look the same every time, so the counts are fair. But every step throws something away, and sometimes that was the part that mattered. The course rule: “clean with purpose, not on autopilot”.

How it works, in plain words

The workflow. A computer cannot read. It can only work with numbers. So every text project follows the same five steps in the same order, like a recipe. A fixed order of steps like this is a workflow. One piece of text you study (one review, one email, one speech) is a document. The whole collection is the corpus.

  1. Text collection: get the text onto your computer, for example by copying it off websites (scraping).
  2. Preprocessing: tidy the text. This is the cleaning described below.
  3. Feature extraction: turn the tidy text into numbers. A feature is one number that describes a document, such as “how many times ‘battery’ appears”. Tools: bag-of-words (counting how often each word appears), TF-IDF (word counts re-weighted so rare, telling words count more) and word embeddings (a list of numbers for each word, so similar words get similar numbers). They only describe the text as numbers; they do not give an answer yet.
  4. Modeling: a method, called a model, uses the numbers to give an answer, such as sorting reviews into “happy” or “unhappy”.
  5. Interpretation and evaluation: check that the answer makes sense, for example by reading some documents yourself.

Short version: collect → clean → features → model → evaluate.

The cleaning steps. The course cleans in a fixed chain, where each step’s output goes into the next, like a factory line. This chain is a pipeline: “lowercase → remove punctuation → tokenize → drop stop words → drop numbers → (stem or lemmatize)”.

  • Lowercasing: turn every capital letter into a small one. Otherwise “War” and “war” count as two different words, and the list of different words gets longer.
  • Removing punctuation: delete marks like . , ! ? so “good.” and “good” become the same.
  • Tokenization: cut the text into pieces, usually single words. Each piece is a token. The list of different words in the whole corpus is the vocabulary.
  • Removing stop words: stop words are very common little words like “the”, “of”, “and” and “is”. They appear in every text, so they rarely help tell texts apart. A ready-made list deletes them. The trap: the standard English list (from NLTK, a free text toolbox) also contains “not”.
  • Removing numbers: delete tokens made only of digits, if you don’t need them.
  • Stemming or lemmatisation (optional): shrink different forms of a word to one base form, so “studies” and “study” count as one word.

Stemming vs lemmatisation. Both shrink words to a base form, in different ways.

  • Stemming chops the ending off by fixed rules, like a pair of scissors. “studies” → “studi”. The result may not be a real word. [Not in course files — general knowledge]
  • Lemmatisation looks the word up, like a dictionary, and returns its dictionary form. “studies” → “study”, “was” → “be”. [Not in course files — general knowledge]
  • Usually stemming gives a smaller but less readable vocabulary. [Not in course files — general knowledge]

N-grams: keeping neighbours together. Counting single words loses the order of the words. “Dog bites man” and “man bites dog” give the same counts. One fix is to count short runs of neighbouring words as one item. A run of n neighbouring words is an n-gram. A 1-gram is one word. A 2-gram, or bigram, is a pair, like “low unemployment”. The course’s point: n-grams “Can handle”low unemployment” versus “high unemployment”“. So n-grams keep a little bit of word order. If you build the pairs before deleting stop words,”not good” also survives as one item.

Searching is a choice too. When you count a keyword, three choices change the number: part of a word or whole word (a substring search for “war” also finds “toward”), case (“War” vs “war”), and whether you divide by text length (normalising by document length, so long texts don’t win by being long). The course calls this a measurement choice: it “can change both the level and the trend of an index” (an index is one number tracked over time).

The four challenges of text as data. The course names four things that make text hard for computers.

  1. Too much text (big data). Billions of tweets need smart storage and many computers working at once, like many cashiers instead of one.
  2. Messy text (noisy, unstructured text). Typos, slang and shortcuts like “gr8”. This needs cleaning, tokenization and normalization: making different spellings count as the same thing (“gud” → “good”).
  3. Several meanings (ambiguity and context). Ambiguity means a word or sentence can mean more than one thing. “Bank” can be a money bank or a river bank. Only the surrounding words, the context, tell you which. The course warns that sentiment analysis (measuring whether a text is positive or negative) “may misinterpret sarcasm or humor”. Sarcasm is saying the opposite of what you mean: “Great, it broke again.”
  4. Ethics (right and wrong). Bias: models copy the unfairness in the text they learn from, as in “Predictive models for hiring based on biased language data in resumes”. Privacy: texts hold personal details, which may be used without consent (the person saying “yes”). Transparency: many models are black boxes, meaning they give answers without showing why.

Why it works: the reasoning

To a computer, “The”, “the” and “THE” are three different things. Cleaning makes the same word look the same, so the counts measure what people wrote about, not accidents of spelling.

But each cleaning step rests on an assumption: “what I throw away does not matter for my question”. Sometimes that is wrong. In 1975 President Ford said “the state of the Union is not good”. After stop-word removal it reads “state union good”. A bad message became a good one. A course notebook cleaned “The US economy is not growing” into “economy growing”, and “Inflation fell from 8% to 3%” into “inflation fell”: the numbers, the main information, were gone.

That is why the course’s main rule is: “Preprocessing should follow the research question.” The research question is the exact question your study tries to answer. For every step, ask: did I delete something my question needs?

When to use it, and when not

  • Use it when you count words: bag-of-words, TF-IDF, keyword or Boolean searches (searches with AND, OR, NOT). Without cleaning, one word gets split into many spellings and the counts are unfair.
  • Use n-grams when meaning sits in a word pair: “low unemployment”, “interest rate”, “not good”.
  • Don’t remove stop words with the standard list when you measure mood (sentiment). It deletes “not” and flips negative into positive.
  • Don’t remove numbers when the numbers are the information, such as inflation rates or prices in a report.
  • Compared with no cleaning: raw text keeps everything, but the same word is counted as many different ones and the vocabulary blows up.
  • Stemming compared with lemmatisation: stemming is cruder and gives a smaller vocabulary; lemmatisation keeps real, readable words. So lemmatisation fits better when people will read the word lists. [Not in course files — general knowledge]
  • Single words compared with n-grams: n-grams keep some order, but they add many more columns to the count table, most of them zero.

What the exam asks

“You build a sentiment index from earnings calls. Which preprocessing step would you switch off, and why?” (An earnings call is a meeting where a company’s bosses talk to investors about results. A sentiment index tracks the mood of texts as one number over time.)

Model answer

I would not remove stop words with the standard list, because it contains “not”. Removing it turns “growth is not strong” into “growth strong” and flips the sentiment (as in Ford’s “not good” example, Interactive 1). I might also keep numbers, since they can carry the main economic information (Practical Day 1). Preprocessing should follow the research question, not be applied on autopilot.

In plain words: The usual stop-word list deletes “not”, so “not strong” becomes “strong”. Pick cleaning steps that fit your question, not out of habit.

“Why is ethics a challenge in automated text processing?” (“Automated text processing” means letting a computer read and judge text for us.)

Model answer

Models learn from training text, so they can pick up the biases in it. For example, a hiring model trained on biased language in resumes can treat candidates unfairly. Text often contains personal data, which raises privacy issues if it is collected without consent. Many models are black boxes, so it is hard to explain why they made a decision.

In plain words: A model copies the unfairness in old text, uses personal data people never agreed to share, and often cannot say why it decided something. Name each problem and its cause.

In the reflection question (2025 Block 5): walk through the workflow in order (collect → clean → features → model → evaluate). Say at which step each pitfall bites. For example, deleting “not” hurts at the cleaning step; ambiguity hurts at the modeling step; bias shows up when you interpret the results.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Saying stop-word removal is “always safe”. It can delete “not” and flip the meaning.
  • Saying that keeping “not” fully fixes bag-of-words. Word order is still lost.
  • Mixing up stemming (chopping endings by rules: “studies” → “studi”) and lemmatisation (dictionary form: “studies” → “study”).
  • Putting TF-IDF or bag-of-words under “modeling”. They belong to feature extraction.
  • Putting lemmatisation under feature extraction. It belongs to preprocessing.
  • Writing “ethics” without saying which kind (bias, privacy or transparency) and why it happens.

Key terms to use

  • workflow: collect, clean, features, model, evaluate
  • preprocessing: cleaning text before counting it
  • tokenization / token: cutting text into words; one piece
  • vocabulary: list of different words in the corpus
  • stop words: very common little words, often deleted
  • stemming: chopping word endings by fixed rules
  • lemmatisation: turning words into their dictionary form
  • n-gram / bigram: neighbouring words kept together; bigram is two
  • measurement choice: a decision that changes what you measure
  • ambiguity / context: several meanings; surrounding words decide which
  • bias, privacy, transparency: the three ethics problems
  • black box: a model whose reasoning you cannot see

True/False traps

  • “Lemmatization is part of the preprocessing step.” → True. Why: lemmatisation tidies each word into its dictionary form before anything is counted, and the workflow above puts all tidying at step 2, preprocessing.
  • “TF-IDF is a modeling technique in the text-as-data workflow.” → False. Why: tf-idf only turns the tidy text into numbers, so in the workflow above it sits at step 3, feature extraction, not at step 4, modeling.
  • “Sarcasm is an example of the ‘noisy text’ challenge.” → False. Why: “noisy text” means messy spelling such as typos, slang and “gr8”; sarcasm is a problem of meaning, so the four challenges above file it under ambiguity and context.
  • “Removing stop words never changes the meaning of a sentence.” → False. Why: the standard stop-word list contains “not”, so Ford’s “the state of the Union is not good” turned into “state union good”, the opposite message.
  • “In the course notebooks, removing English stop words can turn ‘is not good’ into ‘good’.” → True. Why: the standard English stop-word list contains both “is” and “not”, so both are deleted and only “good” is left.
  • “Searching for the letters ‘war’ anywhere in a text counts only the word ‘war’.” → False. Why: a substring search looks for the three letters anywhere, so it also counts words like “toward” and “warm”; only a whole-word search counts “war” alone.
  • “N-grams can distinguish ‘low unemployment’ from ‘high unemployment’.” → True. Why: a bigram keeps the neighbours “low unemployment” together as one item, so it is counted separately from “high unemployment”.
  • “A 2-gram model keeps some information about word order.” → True. Why: each word pair records which word came right before which, so some order survives, although the order of the whole sentence is still lost.
  • “If you do not lowercase, the vocabulary gets smaller.” → False. Why: without lowercasing, “The” and “the” count as two different words, so the vocabulary gets bigger, not smaller.

2. Bag-of-words and the document-term matrix

On the exam: asked in 2025 Q2(a) (5 points). Likely as True/False (word order, sparse vs dense). Useful for the reflection, because keyword searches, tf-idf and simple classifiers all start from this table.

The point of it

A computer can do sums with numbers, not with words. Bag-of-words is the simplest way to turn every text into a row of numbers, with the same columns for every text. Think of a shopping receipt: it says how many of each item you bought, not the order you picked them up.

How it works, in plain words

Picture cutting a text into single words with scissors and throwing them into a bag. Shake the bag. You can still count how many times “price” is in there. But you can no longer read the sentence. Step by step:

  1. Decide what one document is. A document is one unit you count words for. The whole collection is the corpus. A document does not have to be a full article. The course says it “Can be a sentence, a day, an article, a paragraph, etc.” You choose what fits your question.
  2. Clean the text and cut it into words. Each piece is a token.
  3. List every different word in the corpus. This list is the vocabulary. Each item on it is a term: usually one word, sometimes a word pair.
  4. Draw a table. One row per document, one column per term. A table filled with numbers is a matrix, like a spreadsheet that holds only numbers. One box in it is a cell (also called an element).
  5. Fill each cell with a count: how many times this term appears in this document.
  6. Forget the order. Only the counts are kept.

The finished table is the document-term matrix, or DTM: documents in the rows, terms in the columns, counts in the cells. Its size is written “n × p” (read “n by p”): n documents by p terms, where p is the size of the vocabulary. One document’s row is its list of counts. An ordered list of numbers like this is called a vector.

Most cells are zero. The vocabulary holds every word used in any document, but each document uses only a few of them. Imagine a receipt that lists every product in the shop: almost every line says 0. A table that is mostly zeros is sparse. One mostly filled with non-zero numbers is dense. The course says real DTMs are “Typically sparse (many zeros)” and “Typically LARGE (millions of documents, hundred-of-thousands unique terms)”.

Everyday example. A shop makes a DTM of its customer reviews. “The delivery was late, the phone is fine” and “The phone was late, the delivery is fine” get exactly the same row. The table cannot tell them apart.

A fix for word order. The columns can be pairs of neighbouring words instead of single words. A run of n neighbouring words is an n-gram. A 1-gram is one single word, the normal bag-of-words column. A 2-gram column like “low unemployment” keeps a bit of order, so it can tell “low unemployment” from “high unemployment”. What one row stands for (sentence, article, day) and what one column stands for (word or word pair) are both your choices, driven by the research question (the exact question your study tries to answer).

Why it works: the reasoning

The assumption (something a method takes as true to keep things simple) is that word order does not matter. In the course’s words: “The word sequence does not matter!” Each word is counted on its own, as if it had no link to the words around it. In the exam’s language: word occurrences are treated as independent, and context (the surrounding words) is not modelled.

Why that is sensible: for many questions, which words appear and how often tells you most of what you need. An article full of “inflation”, “interest rates” and “central bank” is about the economy, whatever the order. And the method is fast, simple, and gives the same result every time.

The price: anything carried by order or context is lost. “Not good” and “good” look alike once “not” is a separate count. Sarcasm and words with two meanings (“bank”) are invisible.

When to use it, and when not

  • Use it when the question is about what a text talks about: “how often do complaints mention delivery?”, “which articles are about the economy?”.
  • Use it as the starting point for keyword and Boolean searches (searches with AND, OR, NOT), dictionary word lists, tf-idf, comparing documents, and the Naive Bayes classifier (a simple spam-filter-style method), which “operates under the bag-of-words assumption”.
  • Don’t use it when order or context carries the meaning: “not good”, sarcasm, “dog bites man” vs “man bites dog”, or a word with several meanings. A word counts the same in every context, so it cannot handle synonyms (different words, same meaning) or polysemy (one word, several meanings).
  • Be careful when you have few documents but a huge vocabulary, for example about 70 speeches and thousands of word columns. A model that gives each column a weight to predict a number (a regression) then fits the known texts perfectly but predicts new ones badly. This is overfitting, like memorising last year’s exam answers. The fix is regularisation: a brake that stops the model giving big weights to many columns.
  • Compared with n-grams: n-grams keep some order, but they add many more columns and even more zeros.
  • Compared with word embeddings: embeddings give each word a list of numbers so that words with similar meanings get similar numbers. Bag-of-words gives each word its own separate column.

What the exam asks

2025 Q2(a) (5 points): “What is the underlying assumption of bag-of-words methods, and what is the document term matrix?” The official marking guide gives marks for two things: words treated as independent with context not modelled, and documents in rows, words in columns, counts as the entries. Answer both halves.

Model answer

The bag-of-words assumption is that a document can be represented only by which words (tokens) it contains and how often. The order of words and the context they appear in are ignored, so word occurrences are treated as independent of each other. For example, “the economy is not good” and “good, the economy is not” become the same bag.

The document-term matrix \(C\) is the corpus written as numbers under this assumption. It has dimension \(n \times p\): one row for each of the \(n\) documents and one column for each of the \(p\) terms in the vocabulary. Each element \(c_{ij}\) is the count of term \(j\) in document \(i\), so row \(c_i\) is the count vector of document \(i\). In practice it is very large (many documents, hundreds of thousands of terms) and sparse (mostly zeros). What counts as a “document” (sentence, article, day) and as a “term” (single words or n-grams) are choices driven by the research question.

What the symbols mean: \(C\) is the name of the table, \(n \times p\) is its size (n rows by p columns), \(c_{ij}\) is the number in row i, column j, and \(c_i\) is the whole row for document i, like a cell address in a spreadsheet.

In plain words: bag-of-words keeps only “which words, how many times” and throws away the order. The DTM is the spreadsheet of those counts: documents down the side, words across the top, huge and mostly zeros.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Answering only half: the assumption but not the DTM, or the other way round.
  • Mixing up rows and columns (rows = documents, columns = terms).
  • Writing only “it counts words”, with no table size and no mention that order is ignored.
  • Calling the DTM dense. It is sparse (mostly zeros).
  • Forgetting that the vocabulary is far bigger than the number of words in any one document.

Key terms to use

  • bag-of-words assumption: word order ignored; only counts matter
  • independent: each word counted on its own
  • context not modelled: surrounding words are not used
  • document-term matrix: documents down, terms across, counts inside
  • n × p: n documents by p terms
  • vocabulary: list of all different words
  • sparse: most cells in the table are zero
  • corpus: the whole collection of texts
  • document unit: what counts as one “document”
  • n-grams: neighbouring words kept together as one term

True/False traps

  • “In a bag-of-words model the order of words matters.” → False. Why: the bag-of-words assumption throws the order away and keeps only the counts; the course says “The word sequence does not matter!”.
  • “A DTM is typically dense.” → False. Why: each document uses only a few of all the words in the vocabulary, so most cells are zero, which makes the table sparse, not dense.
  • “Most cells in a DTM are zero.” → True. Why: this is the same point from the other side: each text uses few of all the words, and the course says DTMs are “Typically sparse (many zeros)”.
  • “A document-term matrix has dimension n × p, where p is the vocabulary size.” → True. Why: the table has one row for each of the n documents and one column for each of the p different terms, so p is the size of the vocabulary.
  • “With 1-grams, ‘the dog bites the man’ and ‘the man bites the dog’ get the same row in the DTM.” → True. Why: with single-word columns both sentences contain exactly the same words the same number of times, so their rows are identical, like the delivery and phone reviews above; only word-pair columns such as “dog bites” would tell them apart.
  • “A document must always be a full article.” → False. Why: you choose what counts as one document to fit your question, and the course says it “Can be a sentence, a day, an article, a paragraph, etc.”

3. Adjusting the counts: row shares, yes/no (binary), tf-idf

On the exam: asked in 2025 Q2(b) (5 points: three ways, with the motivation for each). Likely as True/False on idf. Useful for the reflection, where tf-idf helps find telling words and similar documents.

The point of it

Plain word counts are unfair in two ways. Long documents win because they are long. Common words win because they are common. Each adjustment fixes one of these, the way “deaths per 100,000 people” stops big countries looking worse only because they are big.

How it works, in plain words

The starting point. A computer first cuts each text into pieces, usually single words; each piece is a token. It then counts how often each word appears in each document and keeps the counts in a table: one row per document (one text), one column per word, each cell a count. This table is the document-term matrix (DTM). The numbers before any change are the raw counts. The slides call raw counts a “Simple representation but sensitive to document length”.

Changing all the numbers in a fixed way so documents become fairer to compare is called normalising the table. The course gives three ways.

Way 1: turn counts into shares (row normalisation). Take one document’s row. Divide every count by the document’s total number of words, its document length. Each cell now says “what share of this document is this word”. This is row normalisation, because each row is adjusted by its own total.

  • Purpose: long documents should not win by length.
  • Everyday example: a 40-page annual report mentions “risk” more often than a one-page press release, even if the press release is all about risk. As shares, the press release correctly comes out as the “riskier” text.

Way 2: turn counts into yes/no (binary). Every count above zero becomes 1; zeros stay 0. A value that can only be 0 or 1, like a light switch, is binary (also called an indicator). A cell now only answers “does this word appear here at all?” The slides: “Ignores frequency; focuses on term occurrence.”

  • Purpose: repeating a word should not count extra.
  • Everyday example: an angry customer who writes “terrible” ten times in one email is still one unhappy customer. Or a lawyer wants to know whether each contract mentions a “termination” clause at all, not how often.

Way 3: weight each count by how rare the word is (tf-idf). Two ingredients:

  • Term frequency (tf): how often the word appears in this document. On the course’s formula slide it is the plain count.
  • Inverse document frequency (idf): a rarity weight. First count in how many documents the word appears at all. That is the document frequency. Careful: it counts documents, not uses. The fewer documents contain the word, the bigger its idf. A word found in every document gets an idf of zero. “Inverse” means “the other way round”: idf goes up when document frequency goes down.

Multiply the two and you get tf-idf: “how often the word is used here” times “how rare it is overall”.

  • Purpose: push down words found everywhere and push up words distinctive to this document (common here, rare elsewhere).
  • Everyday example: in a pile of bank complaints, “bank” and “account” appear in nearly every one, so they tell you nothing. “Overdraft” or “fraud” appear in few, so they tell you what this complaint is about.

A course notebook puts it in one line: “Raw counts describe the language; tf-idf describes the document.” The slides name two uses. Feature selection: “Drop terms with TF-IDF below a threshold”, meaning keep only the useful word columns. Weighting: “Apply TF-IDF scores to emphasize important terms in models.”

One slide mix-up to know. One summary slide uses the name “Term Frequency (TF)” for the share (Way 1), while the formula slide uses the plain count. In the exam, use the plain count inside tf-idf, and call the share “row normalisation”.

Why it works: the reasoning

  • Shares rest on the idea that what a text is about shows in its mix of words, not in how much text there is.
  • Binary rests on the idea that whether a word is used matters more than how often.
  • Tf-idf rests on the idea that a word every document uses cannot help tell documents apart, while a word used a lot here but rarely elsewhere is like a fingerprint. The slides: “Rare terms: High IDF → More significant if term appears in a document.” “Common terms: Low IDF → Less impact on distinguishing documents.” “Balance: TF-IDF balances term frequency with uniqueness.”

When to use it, and when not

  • Use shares when texts differ a lot in length, for example comparing long and short speeches, or tracking a word over years when texts get longer or shorter.
  • Use binary when presence is what matters: does a contract mention a clause, does a review mention delivery.
  • Use tf-idf when you want each document’s most telling words (tagging articles, search), want to compare documents by their distinctive words, or want to drop useless columns before a model.
  • Don’t use binary when “how much” matters: a speech that mentions “war” fifty times and one that mentions it once both get 1.
  • Don’t trust tf-idf blindly: it boosts words that are rare for boring reasons. In one of Trump’s speeches it ranked names of guests and officials near the top, because no other speech mentions them.
  • Software differs: the common Python tool (scikit-learn) adds 1 to every idf, so no word gets exactly zero, and it also rescales each row so length drops out. So “the can survive tf-idf without a stop-word list”. The course: “Software defaults are modelling choices too.”
  • Shares compared with tf-idf: shares fix the length problem but leave “the” and “and” on top. Tf-idf fixes the common-word problem.
  • Tf-idf compared with a stop-word list: both push aside common words. A stop-word list deletes a fixed set of words; tf-idf decides from your own collection how common each word is.

What the exam asks

2025 Q2(b) (5 points): “Describe three ways to normalize the content of the document term matrix. Include in the description the motivation for using each method.” The marking guide rewards each method with its reason. A list of names alone loses marks.

Model answer

  1. Row (length) normalisation: divide each count by the document’s total number of tokens, \(x_i = c_i/m_i\) with \(m_i=\sum_j c_{ij}\), so each row contains word shares. Motivation: long documents have higher counts just because they are longer. Normalising removes the dependence on document length and makes documents of different length comparable.
  2. Binary (indicator) representation: set \(x_{ij}=1\) if term \(j\) occurs in document \(i\) and 0 otherwise. Motivation: it removes the dependence on how many times a word is repeated and focuses on whether a term is used, which is useful when presence matters more than frequency.
  3. Tf-idf: multiply the term frequency by the inverse document frequency, \(\text{tf-idf}_{ij}=c_{ij}\times\log(n/d_j)\), where \(n\) is the number of documents and \(d_j\) the number of documents containing term \(j\). Motivation: it down-weights words that are common across the corpus (a word in every document gets weight 0) and up-weights words that are frequent in this document but rare elsewhere. So it highlights what is distinctive about each document, which is useful for similarity and for feature selection.

What the formulas mean: the first divides each count by the document’s total word count; the second is a switch that is 1 if the word is there and 0 if not; the third multiplies the count by a weight that grows the fewer documents contain the word and is zero when every document contains it.

In plain words: shares, so a long text does not win by length; yes/no, so repetition does not count extra; tf-idf, so words found everywhere count for nothing and words special to this text stand out.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Naming three methods but giving no reason. Half the marks are for the “why”.
  • Giving only two methods when three are asked.
  • Saying tf-idf “removes rare words”. It does the opposite: it boosts them.
  • Defining document frequency as “how often the word appears in total”. It is the number of documents that contain it.
  • Mixing up the slides’ TF (the plain count) with row normalisation (the share) without saying which one you mean.

Key terms to use

  • normalisation: adjusting counts so texts compare fairly
  • raw counts: plain word counts, nothing adjusted
  • row normalisation: each count divided by text length
  • document length: total number of words in a text
  • binary / indicator: 1 if the word appears, else 0
  • term frequency: how often a word is in a text
  • document frequency: number of documents containing the word
  • inverse document frequency: weight that is high for rare words
  • tf-idf: counts re-weighted so rare words count more
  • distinctive vs common words: special to one text vs everywhere
  • feature selection: keeping only the most useful words

True/False traps

  • “A term that occurs in all documents gets idf = 0.” → True (under the slide formula). Why: idf is the rarity weight, and a word found in every document cannot tell documents apart, so under the slide formula its weight is zero. In scikit-learn, which adds 1 to every idf, it would not be exactly zero.
  • “Rare terms get a low idf.” → False. Why: it is the other way round: idf goes up as fewer documents contain the word, so rare words get a high weight, not a low one.
  • “Rare terms receive a high idf.” → True. Why: few documents contain a rare word, so its idf is high; the slides say “Rare terms: High IDF”.
  • “Binary representation keeps information on how often a word is used.” → False. Why: binary turns every count above zero into 1, like a tick box, so a word used once and a word used fifty times both get 1.
  • “Tf-idf removes rare words.” → False. Why: tf-idf keeps every word and only changes its weight: words found everywhere are pushed down, and rare, distinctive words are pushed up.

4. Measuring how similar two texts are: cosine vs Euclidean

On the exam: asked in 2025 Q3(d) (5 points: draw high vs low cosine similarity, in the word-embeddings block). Likely as a “why cosine, not Euclidean?” question and as True/False. Useful for the reflection, whenever a business needs “find the most similar documents”.

The point of it

Cosine similarity is a “same recipe?” test, not a “same amount?” test. A cake recipe for 4 people and one for 12 use different amounts, but the same mix. A good similarity measure should say “same recipe”.

How it works, in plain words

Texts as arrows. First each document (one text) becomes a row of numbers: how often each word appears in it. Sometimes the counts are re-weighted first so that rare, telling words count more; that version is called tf-idf.

Now keep it to two words so you can draw it, say “good” and “bad”. Draw a graph with two axes, the two reference lines of a graph: one going right for “count of bad”, one going up for “count of good”. The corner where both are zero is the origin. A review becomes an arrow that starts at the origin and points towards its mix of “good” and “bad”. A list of numbers drawn as an arrow like this is a vector. A real vocabulary has thousands of words, so thousands of directions. Nobody can draw that, but the computer does the same thing.

Two ways to say how alike two arrows are.

  1. Euclidean distance: put a ruler between the tips of the two arrows. The problem: a long document has bigger counts, so its arrow is longer and its tip sits far out. It ends up far from a short document on the same topic, with the same mix of words.
  2. Cosine similarity: ignore the lengths and look only at the angle between the arrows, how far apart they point. The angle is written θ (“theta”). Arrows pointing the same way mean the same mix of words, whatever their length. The angle is turned into a score: 1 when the arrows point the same way, 0 when they are at a right angle. “At a right angle” is called orthogonal. For word counts, orthogonal means the two texts share no words at all, because counts are never negative. The slides say the score is “bounded between -1 and 1”.

How the computer gets the score without a protractor: it multiplies the matching numbers of the two texts and adds them up (the dot product), then divides by the lengths of both arrows. Dividing by the lengths is what removes the effect of document length. A measure that does not change when a text gets longer or shorter is length-invariant.

Everyday example. Review A says “good good bad”. Review B is review A written twice. Review C says “bad bad good”. By ruler distance, C looks closer to A than B does, although B says exactly what A says. By angle, B points exactly the same way as A (a perfect match, cosine 1), and C points a bit away. When every count of one text is the same multiple of the other’s, like B being A doubled, the counts are called proportional. Proportional texts always point the same way.

Why it works: the reasoning

What a text is about shows in its mix of words, not in how much text there is. Writing a document twice stretches its arrow but does not turn it. So its angle to other documents, and its cosine, stay the same, while its ruler distance to them changes. The slides call the length effect a “Potential issue” of Euclidean distance: a document ends up far away because it “is a longer document and therefore uses all terms more frequently”. They add: “This issue is avoided by using the Cosine similarity measure”.

When to use it, and when not

  • Use cosine when you need “more like this”: recommending similar articles, search, finding complaints like a given one, grouping similar survey answers.
  • Use it for word embeddings too: these are lists of numbers that stand for single words. They are compared with the same cosine, which is why the 2025 drawing question sat in the embeddings block.
  • A course example: the slide “Tf-idf and cosine similarity can be enough!” shows a published finance study (Engle and co-authors, 2020) that built a climate change news index from the Wall Street Journal. It compared each article’s tf-idf with a climate vocabulary using cosine similarity. [Not in course files — general knowledge]
  • Don’t treat a high cosine as proof of the same meaning. It only sees which words overlap. Two texts can use the same words to argue opposite things. Shared speaker, era or writing style can also make texts look alike: the speeches closest to Trump’s 2018 speech were Trump’s 2017 speech and several Obama speeches. The course warns: “Cosine similarity measures overlap in the chosen representation. It is not evidence that the speeches make the same argument.”
  • Compared with Euclidean distance: Euclidean distance measures the gap between arrow tips and grows with document length. Cosine measures only the angle, so length drops out. For comparing documents the course prefers cosine.
  • Cosine on raw counts compared with cosine on tf-idf: with raw counts, words that every text uses make texts look alike. With tf-idf, a word in every document gets weight zero, so only shared telling words count.

What the exam asks

2025 Q3(d) (5 points): “Make an graph illustrating two vectors with high cosine similarity, and two vectors with low cosine similarity.”

What to draw, step by step:

  1. Two axes, with labels such as “count of term a” (up) and “count of term b” (across). They meet at the origin.
  2. Every vector is an arrow starting at the origin. Not dots.
  3. The high pair: two arrows pointing almost the same way, with a small angle between them. Make one clearly shorter, to show that length does not matter.
  4. The low pair: a third arrow pointing a very different way, with a large angle to one of the first two. (The official guide describes three vectors. You may add a fourth for a separate low pair.)
  5. Mark both angles with θ. Next to the small one write “cos close to 1 (high)”, next to the large one “cos low”.
  6. Add one sentence: cosine looks at the angle only, not at length.

Our own drawing. It uses different arrows from the model answer below. Any arrows work, as long as the angles show the idea.

Model answer

Draw two axes (e.g., counts of two terms, or two embedding dimensions) and arrows from the origin.

  • High cosine similarity: A = (6, 1) and B = (4, 1) point in almost the same direction. The angle between them is about 5°, so \(\cos\theta \approx 0.997\), even though A is longer than B. Length does not matter, only direction.
  • Low cosine similarity: A = (6, 1) and C = (1, 6) point in very different directions. The angle is about 71°, so \(\cos\theta = 12/(\sqrt{37}\sqrt{37}) \approx 0.32\). At 90° (no shared terms) the cosine would be 0.

Cosine similarity \(\cos\theta = \frac{a\cdot b}{\|a\|\,\|b\|}\) measures the angle between vectors. That is why it is preferred to Euclidean distance for documents: a long document and a short one with the same word mix are judged similar.

What the symbols mean: A = (6, 1) is a document with 6 uses of the first term and 1 of the second; “embedding dimensions” are two of the numbers in a word’s list of numbers; and the formula says “multiply matching numbers and add them up, then divide by both arrow lengths”, which leaves only the angle.

In plain words: A and B lean the same way, so they are very similar even though A is longer. A and C lean very different ways, so they are not similar. Only direction counts.

Likely: “Why does the course prefer cosine similarity to Euclidean distance for comparing documents?”

Model answer

Euclidean distance measures the straight-line gap between the tips of two document vectors, so a long document is far from a short one even if they use words in the same proportions. Cosine similarity measures only the angle between the vectors, by dividing the dot product by both lengths, so document length drops out. In the slide-22 example, Euclidean distance wrongly says \(d_3\) is closer to \(d_1\) (1.35 vs 1.46), while cosine correctly says \(d_2\) is more similar (0.99 vs 0.84). Cosine is 1 for identical direction and 0 for orthogonal (no shared terms).

The numbers refer to the lecture’s example of three documents, where \(d_1\) has the same word mix as \(d_2\) but is longer. If you forget them, the argument around them still answers the question.

In plain words: the ruler punishes long texts even when they talk about the same things. The angle looks only at the mix of words, so length does not matter.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Drawing dots instead of arrows from the origin.
  • Drawing the “similar” pair with a large angle but tips close together. That shows a small Euclidean distance, not a high cosine.
  • Forgetting to label the axes.
  • Saying a longer document is less similar under cosine. Length does not matter for cosine.
  • Saying cosine 0 means “opposite”. 0 means orthogonal: the documents share no words. −1 would be opposite [Not in course files — general knowledge]. Word counts are never negative, so for them cosine cannot go below 0.

Key terms to use

  • vector: a list of numbers drawn as an arrow
  • origin: the corner where every arrow starts
  • Euclidean distance: straight-line gap between the arrow tips
  • cosine similarity: how closely two arrows point the same way
  • angle θ: how far apart two arrows point
  • dot product: multiply matching numbers, then add them up
  • length-invariant: not changed by how long a text is
  • orthogonal: at right angles; no shared words
  • bounded between −1 and 1: the score always lies there
  • tf-idf + cosine: rarity-weighted counts compared by their angle

True/False traps

  • “Euclidean distance is unaffected by document length.” → False. Why: Euclidean distance is the ruler gap between arrow tips, and a longer document has a longer arrow, so its tip moves further away and the gap grows.
  • “Cosine similarity of 0 means the documents are orthogonal.” → True. Why: a cosine of 0 means the two arrows stand at a right angle, and “at a right angle” is exactly what orthogonal means.
  • “A cosine similarity of 0 means the documents share no terms.” → True for count or tf-idf vectors. Why: as explained above, word counts are never negative, so the arrows can only stand at a right angle when no word appears in both texts.
  • “Two documents whose word counts are exactly proportional have cosine similarity 1.” → True. Why: doubling every count stretches the arrow but does not turn it, so the angle is zero and the score is 1, like reviews A and B above.
  • “A longer document is less similar to a short one under cosine, even with the same word mix.” → False. Why: cosine divides out the arrow lengths and looks only at the angle, so a long and a short document with the same mix of words count as very similar.
  • “Cosine similarity is bounded between −1 and 1.” → True. Why: the slides say the score is “bounded between -1 and 1”. For word counts, which are never negative, it stays between 0 and 1 in practice. [Not in course files — general knowledge]
  • “A high cosine similarity shows that two speeches make the same argument.” → False. Why: cosine only measures how much the texts share the same words, and the course warns it “is not evidence that the speeches make the same argument”.

5. Three ways a computer can learn: supervised, unsupervised, self-supervised

On the exam: asked in 2025 as True/False (f) on LLMs (2 points). Very likely again as True/False (“method X is supervised/unsupervised”), and useful in the reflection, where you should say which kind each of your methods is and why it fits your data.

The point of it

It is a sorting key for every method on the exam. One question decides the box: do we already know what should come out? Get the box right and a whole family of True/False statements becomes easy.

How it works, in plain words

Picture a small online shop with thousands of customer reviews. Each single text we study (one review, one email, one speech) is called a document. There are three situations the shop can be in.

1. Supervised: “we know what comes out.” On this exam the word has two versions.

  • We have the correct answers. Each review comes with the stars the customer gave. That stored answer is a label, and the thing we want to guess is the outcome. The computer studies texts together with their answers and learns the link between them (“reviews that say ‘great’ tend to get more stars”). Then it guesses the answer for new texts. Think of a student who studies past exams with the answer sheet: the answer sheet “supervises” the learning. The examples it learns from are the training data.
  • We wrote the rule ourselves. There are no stars at all, but you decide the rule in advance: “count the happy words, subtract the angry words”. A hand-made word list like this is a dictionary, and a search rule built with AND, OR and NOT is a Boolean search. The teacher puts these in the supervised box too. “Why it works” below explains why.

2. Unsupervised: no answers and no rule. You have only the texts. The computer finds structure on its own: which words show up together, which documents look alike. Only afterwards do you look at the groups and name them. Think of sorting a pile of mixed letters with no instructions, and naming the piles “bills”, “ads”, “love letters” at the end.

3. Self-supervised: the answers come from the text itself. Take a real sentence, hide a word, and ask the computer to guess it. Nobody labelled anything by hand, yet there is a correct answer every time: the hidden word. The teacher calls this self-supervised (the teaching material also uses the name semi-supervised in one place, with the definition “Input (x) and output (y) given by the structure of the training data itself”: the input and the right answer both come from the text). Like making a fill-in-the-blank quiz by covering words in a book: no teacher needed, but always a right answer.

Where each method sits:

  • Boolean search and dictionaries: supervised, because the rule is known.
  • Text regression (OLS, Lasso): supervised. It guesses a number, such as a star rating, from texts that come with that number.
  • Naive Bayes: supervised. It sorts texts into boxes (spam / not spam) after learning from texts that already carry the right box.
  • Topic models (LSA, LDA): unsupervised. They find themes with no answers and no rule.
  • Word embeddings (turning each word into a list of numbers so similar words get similar lists): usually placed in the unsupervised box on this exam. word2vec, one embedding method, learns by guessing hidden words, which is the self-supervised idea.
  • LLMs (large language models, the big text-predicting models behind chatbots): self-supervised in their first, huge training stage. Later fine-tuning stages use examples that people prepared with correct answers, which is openly supervised.

Why it works: the reasoning

Why dictionaries count as “supervised”, even with no answers. This confuses almost everyone. A dictionary method has no answer column and learns nothing from data. So why “supervised”?

Because on this exam “supervised” does not mean “we have labelled data”. It means we know what comes out. A function is a fixed rule that turns an input into an output, like a little machine. A dictionary method is exactly that machine: the input is one document’s word counts, the rule is “happy words minus angry words”, and the output is a score. You wrote the rule before seeing any data, and nothing in it is learned. So for any review anyone hands you, you can work out the score with a pencil and always get the same result. The teacher says the method has a known functional form (“functional form” = the shape of the rule; “known” = fixed by you). In the teacher’s words: “Although we do not know the outcome, we call these methods supervised because we know the functional form.”

What is still missing is the true answer. How happy the writer really is stays hidden. Something real but hidden is called latent. The score is only our estimate of it.

A good way to remember all three boxes: ask who controls what comes out.

  • Supervised with labels: the answer sheet controls it.
  • Dictionary: you control it. You are the supervisor.
  • Unsupervised: nobody controls it. The computer builds its own groups.

Why LLMs are not “unsupervised”. In unsupervised learning there is no right answer. In LLM training there is a right answer for every guess: the real next word. It is only that no human had to write it down. That is why the exam word is “self-supervised”.

[Not in course files — general knowledge] Outside this course, many textbooks use “supervised” only for learning from labelled examples, and some call language-model pre-training “unsupervised”. On this exam, follow the teacher’s use.

When to use it, and when not

  • Use a supervised method with labels when your texts come with the answer you care about (stars, spam/not spam, party). Business case: predict which complaint emails lead to a cancellation, using past emails where you know the outcome.
  • Use a dictionary/Boolean method when you have no labels but know which words signal your concept, and you want a transparent, cheap measure.
  • Use an unsupervised method when you have no labels and do not know the themes in advance (“what do customers talk about?”).
  • Don’t use a supervised learner without labels: it has nothing to learn from. Don’t expect an unsupervised method to measure a specific concept you have in mind: it finds whatever structure is there, and you must name it.
  • The map tells you the kind of method that fits, not the single best one. Real projects often mix boxes, for example finding topics first and then using them as inputs in a regression.

More ideas the exam can test

Two directions: text regression versus Naive Bayes. Both are supervised, but they start from opposite ends. Text regression goes from the words to the outcome. It asks: “this review says ‘great’, so what is the chance of 5 stars?” Naive Bayes, a method that sorts texts into boxes such as spam / not spam, first learns the other way round: which words each box tends to use (“if an email is spam, how likely is the word ‘prize’?”). It then flips that question with Bayes’ rule, a rule of probability that turns “the chance of these words, given the box” into “the chance of the box, given these words”. So a statement saying “regression models the chance of the outcome given the words, Naive Bayes the chance of the words given the class” is True. It does not mean Naive Bayes never picks a box: after the flip, it does.

LSA versus LDA: which one is a chance story? Both are topic models in the unsupervised box: they find themes in texts with no answers given. A probabilistic model works with chances between 0 and 1 that add up to 1, like shares adding up to 100%. A generative model tells a step-by-step story of how the text was written. LDA is both: it imagines each document picks a mix of topics (say 70% sport, 30% money), and each word is drawn from one of those topics. LSA is neither: it is plain table arithmetic that squeezes the word-count table into a few main patterns, and its scores can be any number, even negative. In the teacher’s words: “LSA is not a probabilistic model (non-parametric), and not seen as a plausible generative model for text!” (“Non-parametric” here means it assumes no chance model at all.)

What the exam asks

2025 T/F (f): “Large Language Models are estimated using unsupervised learning.” (2 points)

Model answer

False. The course word is self-supervised. The training lecture says: “Training LLMs is often called self-supervised training. Why? We use the actual text and train the model to predict the next word (or masked words). I.e., no need for manually creating training data.”

In plain words: a near-miss word. Every guess has a right answer (the real next word), so it is self-supervised, not unsupervised.

Likely short question: “What is the difference between supervised and unsupervised text methods? Give one example of each.”

Model answer

In supervised methods we know the outcome (or the mapping) and learn or apply the link between the text and that outcome, like a regression \(y=\beta x+e\); e.g. Naive Bayes learns from speeches labelled by party to classify new speeches. In unsupervised methods there is no outcome; the algorithm finds structure in the text itself, e.g. LDA finds topics (clusters of words) and each document’s topic mix. Dictionary methods are also counted as supervised: there is no observed outcome, but the researcher fixes the function \(f()\), so the output for any input is known.

The formula \(y=\beta x+e\) says: the outcome equals a weight times the input, plus an error for whatever the straight line misses.

In plain words: supervised = we know what comes out (answers to learn from, or a rule we wrote); unsupervised = no answers, the computer finds groups itself.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Calling dictionaries “unsupervised because there is no outcome”. The course still puts them in the supervised box, because the rule is known.
  • Saying dictionaries are supervised “because we observe the outcome”. Right box, wrong reason: we do not observe it.
  • Calling LLM training “unsupervised”. The course word is self-supervised.
  • Putting LSA in the supervised box because its maths looks a bit like regression maths.

Key terms to use

  • supervised: we know what comes out
  • labelled data: texts stored with their correct answers
  • known functional form: the text-to-score rule is fixed in advance
  • unsupervised: no answers; finds groups by itself
  • self-supervised: the answers come from the text itself
  • latent: real but hidden, never observed directly
  • outcome: the thing you want to guess

True/False traps

  • “Dictionary methods are unsupervised, because there is no outcome.” → False. Why: on this exam “supervised” means “we know what comes out”, and with a dictionary we wrote the rule ourselves, so it goes in the supervised box even though there is no outcome.
  • “Boolean and dictionary methods are called supervised because we observe the outcome.” → False. Why: the true outcome (for example how happy the writer really is) stays hidden, or latent; they are supervised only because the researcher wrote the rule in advance.
  • “LDA is an unsupervised method.” → True. Why: LDA is a topic model, and topic models find themes on their own, with no answers and no rule given in advance.
  • “Large Language Models are estimated using unsupervised learning.” → False. Why: every guess in LLM training has a right answer, the real next word, taken from the text itself, so the exam word is self-supervised, not unsupervised. (2025 exam.)
  • “Text regression models the chance of the outcome given the words, while Naive Bayes models the chance of the words given the class.” → True. Why: as “Two directions” above explains, regression goes from the words to the outcome, while Naive Bayes first learns which words each class uses and then flips that round with Bayes’ rule.
  • “LSA is a probabilistic model of how text is generated.” → False. Why: as “LSA versus LDA” above explains, LSA is plain table arithmetic with no chance-based story of how the words were written; LDA is the one that tells that story.

6. Boolean search and the EPU index

On the exam: asked in 2025 as Q2(c) (5 points: describe a Boolean search method, with an example from the course). The EPU index, explained below, is the example to write. Also likely as True/False on the EPU rule.

The point of it

It turns a pile of text into one number you can track over time, using a search rule you write yourself. It is a thermometer for something with no thermometer, such as “how worried is the country about government economic policy?”

How it works, in plain words

Think of a shop website filter: “shoes AND red AND NOT sandals”. A Boolean search works the same way on texts.

  1. Pick the concept you want to measure, for example worry about economic policy. Policy here means what the government decides: taxes, rules, budgets, interest rates.
  2. Pick the key words that signal it. A word you search for is a keyword; your list of keywords is a small dictionary.
  3. Join them with logical operators, also called Boolean operators:
    • AND: both parts must be present;
    • OR: at least one part is enough;
    • NOT: this part must be absent.
    For each document the rule is either true or false. Each single text is a document, here one newspaper article.
  4. Count the hits. A hit is a document that passes the rule. The computer works from the document-term matrix: a big table with one row per document, one column per word, and in each cell how often that word appears in that document.
  5. Normalise. A newspaper that prints twice as many articles gets about twice as many hits, even if nobody is more worried. So you divide the hits by the total number of articles in that period. Normalising means rescaling so numbers can be compared fairly.
  6. Track it over time as an index: one number per month, rescaled to be easy to read, like a consumer confidence index.

A full example: the Economic Policy Uncertainty (EPU) index by Baker, Bloom and Davis (2016). Say you are an economist who wants to know, month by month, how worried America is about what the government will do with the economy. Nobody measures that directly. So the input is millions of US newspaper articles, and the output is one number per month: how much the papers write about uncertainty over economic policy. This is the example the lectures used, so it is the safe one to write in the exam.

  • Three word groups, all required. An article counts only if it has at least one word from each group, in the same article:
    • uncertainty: “uncertain” or “uncertainty”;
    • economy: “economic” or “economy”;
    • policy: “regulation”, “federal reserve”, “deficit”, “congress”, “legislation” or “white house”.
    Inside a group, OR means “any one word is enough”. Between groups, AND means “every group must be there”.
  • The newspapers. Monthly counts from 10 large US newspapers: USA Today, Miami Herald, Chicago Tribune, Washington Post, LA Times, Boston Globe, SF Chronicle, Dallas Morning News, NY Times and Wall Street Journal.
  • The scaling. The monthly counts are scaled and then normalised so that the average month over 1985–2009 equals 100. A month at 200 had twice the usual amount of uncertainty talk.
  • Does it work? The index jumps exactly when you would expect worry: Black Monday, Gulf Wars I and II, 9/11, Lehman/TARP, the Euro crisis and the debt-ceiling dispute (the highest). [Not in course files — general knowledge: Black Monday was the 1987 stock-market crash; Lehman Brothers was a large bank that collapsed in 2008, and TARP was the US bank rescue plan that followed; the debt-ceiling dispute was a 2011 US fight over the government’s borrowing limit.]

Why AND and not OR? An article saying “Economic uncertainty rises as oil prices jump” is about economic uncertainty, but with no policy word it is not about policy uncertainty. The AND makes the rule strict on purpose.

Why it works: the reasoning

The assumption is that how often newspapers write about something tracks how much people care about it. If more articles mention uncertainty, the economy and policy together, worry about policy is likely rising. The spikes at known crises support that the number measures something real.

The teacher calls this a (simple) supervised method. For this exam, “supervised” means “we know what comes out”. Nobody gives us the true level of worry, but we wrote the rule ourselves before seeing any data, so for any article we know whether it counts. In the teacher’s words: “we call these methods supervised because we know the functional form” (the exact rule from text to number is known in advance).

When to use it, and when not

  • Use it when you can name the words that signal your concept, and you need a measure that is cheap, fast (even on millions of articles) and transparent (anyone can check which words were counted). Business case: track the share of customer emails each week that mention “refund” or “cancel”.
  • Don’t use it when the concept is expressed in many different ways you cannot list. It only finds the words you thought of: “doubt about tax policy” is missed, because “doubt” is not on the list.
  • Weak spots: the word choice is subjective (another researcher would pick other words and get another index); it misses synonyms (different words, same meaning, like “unsure” for “uncertain”); it is fooled by polysemy (one word, several meanings, like “bank” as a river bank or a bank for money), because a word counts the same in every context. A news story about a “congress” of doctors would count as a policy hit.
  • Compared with a dictionary (word-list) method: its sister technique. Boolean = a logical rule that a document passes or fails; dictionary = word lists whose hits are counted and combined into a score, such as positive minus negative words.
  • Compared with topic models: a topic model needs no word list and finds themes on its own, but you cannot aim it at one concept you chose in advance.

What the exam asks

Model answer

A Boolean search-based method measures a concept by searching documents for pre-defined key words combined with logical operators (AND, OR, NOT). It starts from the bag-of-words representation (the document-term matrix). The researcher chooses the key words, i.e. a small dictionary, in advance. A document is counted if it satisfies the logical rule. The counts are then aggregated, e.g. per month, and normalised (for example by the total number of articles in that period), so that changes in the volume of text do not drive the index. Because the mapping from text to the measure is fixed by the researcher, the course classifies it as a (simple) supervised technique (“we call these methods supervised because we know the functional form”).

Example — the Economic Policy Uncertainty (EPU) index (Baker, Bloom and Davis, 2016): a newspaper article is counted only if it contains all three elements: (“uncertain” OR “uncertainty”) AND (“economic” OR “economy”) AND at least one policy term (“regulation”, “federal reserve”, “deficit”, “congress”, “legislation” OR “white house”). Monthly counts from 10 large US newspapers are scaled and normalised to mean 100 over 1985–2009. The index spikes at events such as 9/11, Lehman/TARP and the debt-ceiling dispute.

The method is simple, transparent and cheap. But it is subjective (you must know what to search for), and it cannot handle polysemy or synonyms, because a word counts the same in every context.

In plain words: pick signal words in advance, join them with AND/OR/NOT, count the articles that match, and divide by the total so busy news months are not unfairly high. The EPU index does this with uncertainty + economy + policy words in US newspapers, and it jumps during big crises. It is easy and cheap, but it depends on your word choices and cannot tell different meanings of a word apart.

  • Inventing your own example when the question asks for an example from the course.
  • Claiming the method handles polysemy or synonyms well.

Key terms to use

  • Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT): words that combine search terms
  • keyword list / dictionary: the fixed words you search for
  • count and normalise: count matches, divide by the total
  • EPU index: monthly newspaper measure of policy uncertainty
  • Baker, Bloom and Davis (2016): the economists who built EPU
  • known functional form: the text-to-number rule is fixed
  • subjective: depends on the researcher’s own choices

True/False traps

  • “An article enters the EPU count if it contains ‘uncertainty’ and ‘economy’, even without a policy term.” → False. Why: the three EPU word groups are joined with AND, so an article only counts if it has an uncertainty word, an economy word and a policy word; “Economic uncertainty rises as oil prices jump” is left out.
  • “Boolean search methods are called supervised because we know the functional form.” → True. Why: the researcher writes the search rule before seeing any data, so for any article we know in advance whether it counts; that known rule is what “supervised” means on this exam.
  • “Boolean search methods are unsupervised, because there is no outcome.” → False. Why: nobody gives us the true level of worry, but the rule is fixed by the researcher, and for this exam a known rule is enough to call a method supervised.

7. Dictionary (word-list) methods and their weaknesses

On the exam: asked in 2025 as True/False (h) on polysemy (2 points). Likely again as True/False on weaknesses, and very useful in the reflection: a sentiment word list is an obvious first method for customer texts, and the examiner wants its pitfalls named.

The point of it

It is a marking sheet for text. You write a column of “good words” and a column of “bad words”, tick every match in a text, and the score says how positive or negative the text sounds. The teacher calls Boolean/dictionary methods “by far the most used approach in economics and finance”, because they are simple, cheap and easy to check.

How it works, in plain words

Say a shop wants to score each review as happy or unhappy.

  1. Choose word lists. One list of positive words (good, great, …) and one of negative words (bad, wasted, …). A hand-made list like this is a dictionary. You can use a ready-made one or build your own:
    • General Inquirer: a ready-made, general-purpose list of positive and negative words, built for everyday text.
    • Loughran and McDonald (2011): a list made specially for finance texts.
  2. Count the hits in each document. Each single text (one review, one speech) is a document. A hit is a word in the text that is on your list.
  3. Combine the counts into a score. The usual one is tone (or sentiment): how positive or negative a text sounds, measured as positive hits minus negative hits.
  4. Normalise for length. Divide by the number of words in the text, so a long speech does not look more emotional only because it is long. Normalising means rescaling so texts can be compared fairly.

The Boolean method is the sister technique: instead of counting hits on lists, a document passes or fails a search rule built with AND, OR and NOT.

Does counting words work at all? Research shows that it does: the tone of news predicts firms’ stock returns (Tetlock et al. 2008); the tone of the words in the Greenbook forecasts tells you more about future economic growth than the forecast numbers alone (Sharpe et al. 2017) [Not in course files — general knowledge: the Greenbook forecasts are made by staff at the US central bank, the Federal Reserve]; the tone of central banks’ financial stability reports moves stock markets (Born et al. 2014).

Why it works: the reasoning

The assumption is that the words a writer picks reveal their attitude: more happy words, happier writer. The true mood is hidden (the word for this is latent), and the score is our estimate of it.

The teacher puts dictionary methods in the supervised box. On this exam “supervised” means “we know what comes out”. There are no correct answers to learn from, but you wrote the rule (“positive minus negative, divided by length”) before seeing any data. So for any text, anyone gets the same score. The rule has a known functional form (its shape is fixed by you).

The method treats each text as a bag of words: a bag of loose words with their order thrown away. That is the source of most of its weaknesses.

When to use it, and when not

  • Use it when you know which words signal your concept, you have no labelled examples, and you need something cheap, fast and transparent: you can always see which words gave the score. Business case: a first, quick read of customer mood across thousands of survey answers.
  • Don’t use it when meaning depends on context, field-specific language or word order. Its weaknesses, each with an everyday example:
    • Polysemy: one word, several meanings. “Bank” can be a river bank or a bank for money. “Apple” can be the fruit or the company. A word list counts the spelling, never the meaning, so it counts “bank” the same in “river bank” and “bank loan”. It is like a metal detector that beeps at bottle caps and coins alike.
    • Synonyms: different words, same meaning. “Car” and “automobile”, or “unsure”, “doubt” and “uncertain”. If the list has one and not the other, the missing ones are never counted.
    • Field-specific words. In a firm’s annual report (a 10-K), words like liability, tax and cost are normal business words, not bad news. An everyday list still marks them negative, so the score comes out wrong. That is why Loughran and McDonald built a finance list. [Not in course files — general knowledge: a 10-K is the annual report US listed firms file with the SEC.]
    • Negation. “No crisis” is good news, but a plain count only sees “crisis”. A crude fix is to skip a hit when the word right before it is not, no, never, without or nor. “Not a crisis” still slips through, because the word before “crisis” is “a”.
    • Very subjective. Someone chooses the words; another researcher would choose others and get a different answer.
    • You must know what to search for. You only find what you thought of in advance.
    • One frequent word can become the index. A very common word on the list can produce most of the hits. Always check which words drive the score.
  • Compared with topic models: topic models need no word list and can partly handle synonyms and polysemy, because they look at which words appear together. But they find themes, not a score for a concept you chose.
  • Compared with contextual embeddings (word number-lists that change with the surrounding sentence, so “bank” next to “river” differs from “bank” next to “loan”): they handle polysemy, which a word list cannot.
  • Compared with a supervised classifier such as Naive Bayes: the classifier learns which words matter from labelled examples, so it is less dependent on your guesses, but it needs those labels.

What the exam asks

Model answer

The course lists polysemy as an issue for Boolean/dictionary methods, together with synonyms and the fact that they are “very subjective” and you “need to know what to search for”. Topic models and contextual embeddings are the course’s partial fixes.

In plain words: the statement sells a weakness as a strength. A word list cannot tell the two meanings of “bank” apart, so it is False.

8. Text regression and regularisation (Lasso, cross-validation, out-of-sample)

On the exam: asked in 2025 as True/False (e) (2 points: regularisation and the curse of dimensionality). Likely again as True/False on the penalty dial and in-sample scores, or as a short “why do we regularise?” question. Useful in the reflection whenever you want to predict a number from text.

The point of it

It predicts a number from the words in a text, such as a review’s star rating from its words. The catch is that there are far more words than texts, so the model can “memorise” instead of learn. Regularisation is the brake that stops it.

How it works, in plain words

Step 1: regression, from zero. Think of predicting a house’s price from its size. Put every past sale on a chart: size along the bottom, price up the side. The dots slope upwards, because bigger houses cost more. Now draw the straight line that passes as close to all the dots as possible. For a new house, find its size on the bottom, go up to the line, and read off the price. That is a regression: predicting a number by drawing the best line through points.

  • The number you predict (price) is the outcome.
  • What you predict from (size) is the input, also called the explanatory variable or the regressor.
  • The coefficient (or weight) says how much the input matters: how much the price goes up for each extra square metre.
  • The standard way to pick the best line is OLS (ordinary least squares): it chooses the line whose misses, the gaps between the dots and the line, are as small as possible in total.

You can use many inputs at once (size, rooms, age), each with its own coefficient.

Step 2: words as inputs. Each single text (one speech, one review) is a document. Count how often each word appears in each document and put the counts in a table: one row per document, one column per word. This is the document-term matrix. In a text regression, every word column is one input with its own coefficient. A word with a positive coefficient pushes the prediction up each time it appears; a negative one pushes it down. Because the method learns from texts whose right number is already known, it is supervised.

An example. Say you want to know whether the words in a US president’s yearly speech to Congress (the State of the Union speech) can predict how fast the economy grew that year. The input is the word counts of each speech; the output is that year’s growth. You have about 70 such speeches, each paired with its year’s growth. This is the example the lectures used.

Step 3: the problem, more words than documents. 70 speeches, but thousands of different words. Textbooks write the number of documents as n and the number of different words as p. “n ≪ p” is shorthand for “n is much smaller than p”: far fewer documents than words, which is the normal case for text. “n ≫ p” would mean the opposite, far more documents than words, which is rare for text. Every coefficient is an unknown the computer must choose, and every document gives only one fact to check it against. With more unknowns than facts, the computer can always find coefficients that hit every training speech exactly, in endless different ways.

That sounds great, but it is memorising, not learning. Think of a student who memorises last year’s exam answers. They score 100% on those exact questions and fail a new exam. In the model this is overfitting: it memorises the training examples, including their random noise, and then predicts badly on new ones. Each input column is one dimension, so the trouble that comes with far more columns than rows is called the curse of dimensionality. In the teacher’s words: “we have more explanatory variables than observations of the outcome (more unknowns than equations). Thus, we cannot apply linear regression directly.”

Step 4: the fix, charge a fee. Imagine guessing house prices from 70 sales, but you may use 5,000 features (door colour, the dog’s name…). With enough features you can explain the 70 prices exactly, yet predict the next house badly. So you give the model a strict budget: every feature it uses costs money, so it only keeps the few that pay for themselves.

In the regression this budget is a fee on big coefficients. The model now tries to keep “misses + fee” small, so a word only gets a big coefficient if it cuts the misses by more than its fee. Adding the fee is regularisation (or penalisation); the fee is the penalty; pulling coefficients towards zero is shrinkage. Versions of the fee (“Lasso and friends”):

  • Lasso can push many coefficients to exactly zero. Those words drop out, leaving a short, readable list of words that matter.
  • Ridge shrinks coefficients towards zero but never exactly to zero, so it keeps every word. [Not in course files — general knowledge]
  • Elastic net mixes the two.

Step 5: the dial, λ (lambda). The strength of the fee is a dial called λ.

  • λ = 0: no fee, so you are back to plain OLS. In the teacher’s words: “This is typically not what we do with text data!”
  • Small λ: a tiny fee; the model can still overfit.
  • Big λ: a huge fee; every coefficient is pushed to zero, the model ignores all words and predicts the average every time.

Step 6: test on texts the model has never seen. To set λ, hide part of the data. Let the model learn from the rest (the training set) and check how well it predicts the hidden part (the test set, or held-out data). Try several λ values and keep the one that predicts the hidden part best. This is cross-validation, a form of out-of-sample testing (“out-of-sample” = measured on data the model never saw). A common way: cut the speeches into 5 groups, train on 4, predict the 5th, and repeat so each group is predicted once. A second way to pick λ is an information criterion such as AIC or BIC: a score on the training data that rewards good fit but charges for every extra coefficient [Not in course files — general knowledge].

Step 7: judging the result. The usual score is R²: 1 means perfect predictions, 0 means no better than always guessing the average. Measured on the training documents it is in-sample R²; on hidden documents it is out-of-sample R².

Why it works: the reasoning

The insight is that a model that can fit anything proves nothing. With far more words than documents, the in-sample R² is close to 1 almost always. A placebo test shows this. A placebo is a fake treatment, like a sugar pill. Here you shuffle the growth numbers between years, so the words cannot hold real information about them. The in-sample R² still stays near 1, like a student memorising 70 random answers. The out-of-sample R² collapses. The lesson, in the teacher’s words: “Judge text models on data they have not seen.”

The penalty works because most words carry no real signal. Forcing the model to “pay” for each word makes it spend only on words that genuinely help predict new texts. That is learning instead of memorising.

When to use it, and when not

  • Use it when each text comes with a number you want to predict or explain: star ratings, sales, share-price moves, economic growth. Business case: predict a review’s rating, or which words go with low satisfaction scores. Lasso is good when you want a short list of the words that matter.
  • Don’t use it when the outcome is a category (spam / not spam): use a classifier such as Naive Bayes. Be careful with few texts: even the best model may predict poorly, and the honest score can be near or below zero.
  • Watch out: a different λ gives a different list of words. If a vendor shows “R² = 0.95”, ask: in-sample or out-of-sample?
  • Compared with dimension reduction: instead of a fee, you can first squeeze the thousands of word columns into a few summary columns (PCA, Partial Least Squares, or topic shares) and run the regression on those. In the teacher’s words: “Text regressions are high-dimensional problems. Thus, we need penalization methods or dimension reduction.”
  • Compared with a dictionary score: a dictionary needs no labelled data, but you guess which words matter; a text regression lets the data decide, but needs the outcome for every training text.

What the exam asks

2025 T/F (e): “In text regressions usage of regularization techniques help overcome the curse of dimensionality.” (2 points)

Model answer

True. A text regression predicts a number (like GDP growth) from word counts. Regularization means adding a penalty that keeps the word weights small. The curse of dimensionality is the problem of having far more columns (words) than rows (documents), so the model can fit anything.

In plain words: with far more words than documents, the model can bend itself to fit anything; the fee on big word weights stops that. There is no trick, only clumsy grammar (“usage … help”).

Likely short question: “Why do we need regularisation in text regressions, and how is the penalty chosen?”

Model answer

In a text regression the regressors are the columns of the document-term matrix, so we usually have far more words than documents (\(n \ll p\)), i.e. more unknowns than equations, and OLS cannot be estimated or fits the training data perfectly but meaninglessly (the curse of dimensionality). Penalised regressions such as Lasso add a cost \(n\lambda\sum_j\omega_j|\beta_j|\) for large coefficients, which shrinks most of them towards zero (Lasso sets some exactly to zero), so only informative words keep weight; λ = 0 gives back OLS. The penalty λ is chosen by cross-validation: estimate the model on training data for different λ values and pick the one that predicts held-out test data best (or use an information criterion such as AIC/BIC).

In words: \(n \ll p\) means far fewer documents than words, and the cost term means “add up the size of every word’s weight, each with its own small cost factor, and multiply by the dial λ and the number of documents”.

In plain words: too many words for too few documents lets plain regression memorise; a fee on big word weights keeps only the useful words, and the fee’s size is set by testing on held-back texts.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Saying there are more documents than words. It is the other way round.
  • Saying λ = 0 is “maximum penalty”. It is no penalty at all.
  • Trusting in-sample R² (the score on the training documents).
  • Claiming Ridge sets weights to exactly zero. It only shrinks them; Lasso is the one that sets some to zero.

Key terms to use

  • text regression: predicting a number from word counts
  • curse of dimensionality: far more words than documents
  • overfitting: memorises training data, fails on new
  • regularisation / penalty: a fee for big word weights
  • Lasso: penalty that can drop words entirely
  • Ridge: penalty that shrinks but keeps all words
  • λ (lambda): dial for how strong the fee is
  • cross-validation: repeatedly test on held-back documents
  • out-of-sample R²: fit score on unseen documents
  • placebo test: shuffle the answers; honest score should collapse

True/False traps

  • “In text regressions usage of regularization techniques help overcome the curse of dimensionality.” → True. Why: with far more words than documents the model can fit anything (the curse of dimensionality), and the fee on big word weights (regularisation) stops it from doing so. (2025 exam.)
  • “With λ = 0 the Lasso equals OLS.” → True. Why: λ is the dial for the fee; at zero there is no fee, so only the fit counts, which is plain OLS (Step 5).
  • “In Lasso, high values of λ make coefficients larger.” → False. Why: turning the λ dial up makes the fee bigger, which shrinks the coefficients; a big enough fee pushes them all to zero (Step 5).
  • “λ is chosen by cross-validation.” → True. Why: as Step 6 explains, you try several λ values and keep the one that predicts the held-back documents best; that is cross-validation.
  • “A high in-sample R² proves the text predicts the outcome.” → False. Why: in-sample R² is measured on the texts the model learned from, and with far more words than documents the model can fit those almost perfectly anyway; only the score on unseen texts is honest.
  • “If a Ridge regression of a shuffled (placebo) target on word counts gives an in-sample R² near 1, the words are informative.” → False. Why: as the placebo test above shows, shuffled answers carry no real information, yet the model still memorises them; only the out-of-sample R² would reveal that it has learned nothing.
  • “Text regressions typically have more documents than words (n ≫ p).” → False. Why: as Step 3 explains, text usually gives far fewer documents than words (n ≪ p), not the other way round.

9. Naive Bayes: sorting texts into categories

On the exam: asked in 2025 as True/False (g) (2 points: Naive Bayes is not for two continuous variables). Likely again as True/False or a short question on why it is “naive” and “Bayes” and what smoothing does. Useful in the reflection whenever texts must be sorted into groups.

The point of it

It is a spam filter. It learns from old emails that are already marked “spam” or “not spam” which words are typical for each box, then lets the words of a new email “vote” on which box it belongs in.

How it works, in plain words

Sorting, not guessing a number. Some tasks ask for a number (next year’s growth). That is the job of regression. Other tasks ask you to put something into one of a few boxes: spam or not spam, Democrat or Republican, good or bad review. That is classification; the method doing it is a classifier; each box is a class (or category). A number that can take any value, like height or price, is a continuous outcome. One of a fixed set of boxes is a categorical outcome. Naive Bayes is for categorical outcomes.

Think of a doctor deciding between a cold and the flu. They know how common each illness is right now, and how often each symptom comes with each illness. For a patient with fever and aching muscles, they weigh “how common the illness is” together with “how typical these symptoms are for it” and pick the more likely illness. Naive Bayes does the same, with words as the symptoms.

Step by step, for a spam filter:

  1. Collect labelled examples. Old emails that already carry the right box. The box written next to each is its label; this pile is the training set. Because it learns from answers, it is supervised.
  2. How common is each class? If a quarter of old emails were spam, your starting guess for a new email is “a quarter chance of spam”. This starting guess is the prior.
  3. How typical is each word for each class? “Prize” is common in spam and rare in normal mail; “meeting” the other way round. How typical a word is for a class is its likelihood.
  4. Fix the zero problem. If a word never appeared in spam, its likelihood in spam would be zero, and one zero wipes out all the other evidence: the class can never win, however strong the rest of the email points to it. So we pretend every word was seen one extra time in every class. This is Laplace smoothing: add 1 to every count. Think of it as giving every word one free “starter” sighting, so one missing word cannot veto everything else.
  5. Score a new email for each class. Combine the prior with how typical each of the email’s words is for that class. Every word counts as separate evidence.
  6. Pick the winner. The class with the highest score wins. The chance of a class after reading the email is the posterior: prior + evidence from the words → posterior.

The course notebook sums it up: “Naive Bayes learns, for each party, the odds of every word, and lets the words vote.”

Testing it fairly. Check it on texts it did not learn from (the test set), and compare with a lazy rule such as “always guess the biggest class” (the majority-class baseline). Beware data leakage: test information sneaking into training. In the course, the notebook guesses a US president’s party from their speeches. With a random split, the same president appears in training and test, so the model can recognise the president and era (names, events) rather than the party. Holding out whole presidents (a grouped split) gives an honest test.

Why it works: the reasoning

Why “Bayes”. Bayes’ rule is a rule of probability that lets you flip a question. From the training data we learn “how likely are these words, if the email is spam?” Bayes’ rule turns that into the question we care about: “how likely is it spam, given these words?” The slides: “It is called ‘Bayes’ because it applies the Bayes rule to make inference” (inference = drawing a conclusion from data).

Why “naive”. It treats every word as separate evidence, as if, once you know the class, one word tells you nothing about the next. The slides: “It is called ‘Naive’ because it operates under the bag-of-words assumption and assumes that the words in a document are independent.” The bag-of-words assumption treats a document as a loose bag of words: order does not matter, only counts. This is clearly false in real text (“New” is often followed by “York”), which is why it is “naive”. It is not called naive because it is simple.

Why it still works. For sorting, you only need to know which class scores highest, not the exact chances. Even with the naive assumption, typical words push in the right direction. The slides say it “works very well for categorizing documents” and “is usually regarded as a good starting point”.

When to use it, and when not

  • Use it when you have texts with known categories and want to sort new ones: spam filtering, sorting complaint emails by type, tagging reviews as good or bad, speeches by party. It is fast, needs only counting, and you can see which words push a text towards each class.
  • Don’t use it when the outcome is a number, such as a price or growth rate: use regression. Don’t use it without labelled examples: it has nothing to learn from. Be careful when word order or phrases carry the meaning (“not good”), since it ignores order.
  • Watch out: a random train/test split can leak information and make accuracy look better than it is. A complaint flagger trained on a random split of last year’s mail may fail on next year’s mail.
  • Compared with text regression: both are supervised. Regression predicts a number from the words; Naive Bayes picks a category, and it goes the other way first (it learns which words each class uses, then flips it with Bayes’ rule).
  • Compared with a dictionary method: a dictionary needs no labels, but you guess which words matter; Naive Bayes learns which words matter from labelled examples.
  • Other classifiers named in the course: Support Vector Machines, logistic regression and neural networks.

What the exam asks

2025 T/F (g): “The Naive Bayes Classifier is great for capturing the relationship between two continuous variables.” (2 points)

Model answer

False. Naive Bayes is a classifier for a categorical outcome (an answer that is one of a few groups, like “spam / not spam” or “party a / party b”), and it works on word counts. Continuous variables are numbers that can take any value, like height or GDP growth.

In plain words: a method used for the wrong job. Naive Bayes sorts things into boxes; linking two numbers (like height and weight) is the job of regression.

Likely short question: “How does Naive Bayes classify a document, and why is it called ‘naive’ and ‘Bayes’?”

Model answer

From labelled training documents, Naive Bayes estimates the prior share of each class (\(n_k/n\)) and, for each class, the probability of each word (its smoothed frequency among all words in that class, with +1/+p Laplace smoothing so unseen words do not give zero). For a new document it multiplies the class prior by the probabilities of all the document’s words and picks the class with the largest result. It is “Bayes” because it uses Bayes’ rule to turn \(p(\text{words}\mid\text{class})\) into \(p(\text{class}\mid\text{words})\), and “naive” because it uses the bag-of-words assumption that words are independent given the class. It is for categorical outcomes (spam/not spam), not continuous ones.

In words: \(n_k/n\) is the share of training documents in each class; “+1/+p” means add 1 to each word count and add the number of different words to the total; and the two \(p(\ldots)\) terms read “the chance of the words given the class” and “the chance of the class given the words”.

In plain words: it learns how common each group is and how often each word shows up in each group (with 1 added so nothing is zero). For a new document it combines these and picks the most likely group. “Bayes” is the rule that flips the question round; “naive” is the belief that words do not depend on each other.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Using Naive Bayes for continuous outcomes (numbers like GDP growth).
  • Saying “naive because it is simple”. It is naive because of the independence assumption.
  • Forgetting smoothing, or forgetting the prior (how common each class is).
  • Reporting accuracy from a random split as proof that the model learned “party”. It may have only learned names and events of each president.

Key terms to use

  • classification: sorting texts into groups
  • categorical outcome: the answer is a group, not a number
  • prior: how common each class is beforehand
  • likelihood: how typical the words are for a class
  • posterior: chance of a class after reading the words
  • Bayes’ rule: flips “words given class” into “class given words”
  • conditional independence: words treated as unrelated within a class
  • Laplace smoothing: add 1 so no chance is zero
  • majority-class baseline: always guess the most common class
  • data leakage: test information sneaking into training

True/False traps

  • “The Naive Bayes Classifier is great for capturing the relationship between two continuous variables.” → False. Why: it sorts into categories; linking two numbers is regression’s job. (2025 exam.)
  • “Naive Bayes is called naive because it assumes the words in a document are independent (bag-of-words).” → True. Why: treating words as unrelated is the naive assumption behind the name.
  • “Laplace smoothing adds 1 to every word count so that unseen words do not give zero probability.” → True. Why: no word can then have a chance of exactly zero and veto the rest.
  • “Naive Bayes models the chance of the words given the class, and uses Bayes’ rule to get the chance of the class given the words.” → True. Why: that flip is the “Bayes” in the name.
  • “Naive Bayes is an unsupervised method.” → False. Why: it learns from texts that already carry their correct class.

10. Topic models: LSA and LDA

On the exam: asked in 2025 as Q2(d) (5 points: what topic models are, two methods, their intuition, difference and similarity). “LSA is not probabilistic” is a natural True/False. Very useful in the reflection for “what do customers talk about?”

The point of it

It finds the themes in a pile of texts that nobody has read or tagged, and says how much of each theme is in each text. Think of 5,000 open survey answers: a topic model tells you “these are the 6 things people talk about, and this answer is mostly about delivery, a little about price.”

How it works, in plain words

Step 1: turn texts into counts. Each single text (one article, one survey answer) is a document. The computer stores how often each word appears in each document in a big table, one row per document and one column per word: the document-term matrix. Most cells are zero.

Step 2: the trick, words of one theme travel together. “Goal” and “match” tend to appear in the same articles; so do “stock” and “market”. So a topic is a cluster of words that tend to appear together, such as {motor, toyota, nissan, car, auto…}. A topic model hunts for groups of words whose counts rise and fall together across documents.

Step 3: each document is a mix of topics. The course example is a news story about Nissan stopping part of its production because a volcanic eruption in Iceland stopped it flying in car parts. It is partly “Motor”, partly “Natural disasters” and partly “Outlook”. So a document is a mixture of topics, in different amounts. Think of a smoothie bar: each smoothie (a document) is a mix of a few base fruits (the topics), and each fruit has its own taste made of many flavour notes (the words). You only taste the finished smoothies; the model works out the fruits and each smoothie’s recipe.

Step 4: you choose how many topics, K. The computer does not know the “right” number. The topics are latent (hidden): you never see a topic, only the words it leaves behind. And the computer only hands you lists of words with numbers: you look at each topic’s top words and give it a name. The names are your interpretation.

Because nobody gives the computer the themes or any example answers, topic models are unsupervised.

Way 1: LSA (Latent Semantic Analysis), squeezing the table. LSA hands the word-count table to a standard maths procedure, SVD (Singular Value Decomposition), that squeezes it into its few main patterns. Think of a school report with ten grades per student, summarised by two scores (“how strong overall” and “maths-type versus language-type”). SVD does the same for the word table: it finds the patterns that describe the table best, gives each document a score on each pattern (the factors) and each word a weight on each pattern (the loadings). You keep the K strongest patterns and throw the rest away as noise. LSA is the same as PCA (Principal Components Analysis, a standard way of finding the few directions in which data differ most) applied to text. LSA’s scores are plain numbers: they can be negative and do not add up to anything, so they are not shares and not chances.

Way 2: LDA (Latent Dirichlet Allocation), a story of how documents are written. LDA works with probabilities: chances from 0 (never) to 1 (certain), where a full list of chances adds up to 1, like shares adding to 100%. LDA imagines every article was written like this:

  1. Give the article a topic mix, for example 80% finance and 20% football.
  2. For each word: spin a wheel to pick one topic from that mix; then pick a word from that topic’s own list of word chances.

A model that tells such a step-by-step story of how the data were produced is a generative model. Nobody thinks real writers work like this, but the story has the right shape: “Topics are distributions of words” and “Documents are distributions of topics”. The computer then runs the story backwards: which topic word lists, and which topic mix per article, best explain the counts we actually see?

LDA also starts with a built-in assumption, the Dirichlet prior (a prior is what a model assumes before seeing data): each document uses only a few main topics, and each topic has only a few main words. This pattern of “a few big shares, most near zero” is sparsity. Two dials control it: α for the document side (lower α means fewer main topics per document) and β for the topic side (lower β means fewer main words per topic).

Why it works: the reasoning

The assumption is that words that belong to one theme appear in the same documents, and that each document blends a few themes. If that holds, the thousands of word columns can be summarised by a handful of topics. That brings four benefits: it is easier to understand (6 themes, not 10,000 words), less noisy (one-off words get washed out), it can partly handle synonyms (different words, same meaning) and polysemy (one word, several meanings), and it gives a few clean columns to use as inputs in a later regression (a second-stage analysis), which avoids having more word columns than documents.

The difference. The lecture’s key line: “LSA is not a probabilistic model (non-parametric), and not seen as a plausible generative model for text!” LSA is table arithmetic with no story of how words were written. LDA is a chance story, so its numbers are real shares that add up to one, which makes it easier to read and a more believable model of text. LDA is popular because it sorts texts “in much the same manner as humans would do”.

The similarity. Both shrink the big table into a few topics, both give a “documents → topics” part and a “topics → words” part, in both you choose K, and in both you name the topics.

Choosing K. The lecture: “Ad-hoc or based in out-of-sample testing (marginal likelihood)! For LSA one can look at the variance explained.” In plain words: by judgement; or by holding back some texts, fitting the model on the rest, and checking how well it explains the held-back ones; or, for LSA, by how much of the table each pattern accounts for.

Stability. LDA starts from a random guess and improves it round after round. The starting point is set by a random seed. A different seed, or a different K, can give different topics. So run it again with another seed and trust the themes that come back each time, before using topics as data.

When to use it, and when not

  • Use it when you have many texts, no labels, and want to know what they are about: survey answers, complaints, news archives, central-bank meeting notes. Also to turn texts into a few columns for a later regression.
  • Don’t use it when you need to measure one concept you chose in advance (use a dictionary or Boolean search) or predict a known outcome (use a supervised method). Don’t feed it re-weighted word scores such as tf-idf: LDA models plain word counts.
  • LSA vs LDA: pick LDA when you want readable shares (“this answer is 70% delivery”); LSA’s numbers can be negative and cannot be read as shares.
  • Compared with dictionaries: no word list needed and less dependent on your guesses, but the themes are whatever the data contain, not what you asked for.

What the exam asks

2025 Q2(d): “In the context of what you have learned in this course - what is meant by topic models? Mention at least two methods for estimating topics and the underlying intuition and difference/similarity of these methods.” (5 points)

Model answer

In this course a topic is a cluster of words that tend to occur together, and topic models are unsupervised methods that explain the large, sparse document-term matrix \(C\) (\(n\times p\)) with a much smaller number \(K\) of latent topics. Each document is assumed to be a mixture of topics with different weights, and each topic puts different weights on the words in the vocabulary. This reduces dimension (\(K \ll p\)), makes interpretation easier, reduces noise, can partly handle synonyms and polysemy, and gives variables that can be used in second-stage analysis.

Method 1 — LSA (PCA via the Singular Value Decomposition): decompose \(C = USV'\) and keep the first \(K\) components, giving document factors \(F=U_{1:K}S_{1:K}\) and word loadings \(L = V_{1:K}\). Intuition: find the few directions that explain most of the variation in word use. It is a matrix-decomposition (non-probabilistic) method, and the factor values are unrestricted numbers.

Method 2 — LDA (Latent Dirichlet Allocation, Blei et al. 2003): a probabilistic generative model. For each document draw a topic distribution; then for each word draw a topic from it and a word from that topic’s word distribution. Topics are probability distributions over words, and documents are probability distributions over topics. Dirichlet priors make each document use few topics and each topic use few high-probability words.

Similarity and difference: both reduce \(C\) to \(K\) latent factors with a document-by-topic and a topic-by-word matrix (“a probabilistic topic model is just a factor model for multinomial data”), and in both the number of topics \(K\) is chosen by the analyst and the topic labels are the analyst’s interpretation. The difference is that SVD/LSA is linear algebra with unrestricted, orthogonal factors, while LDA is a probability model whose weights are non-negative and sum to one. That makes LDA a more plausible model of how text is generated and easier to read as “shares”. The cost is that LDA estimates can change with the random seed, so stability should be checked.

In words: \(C\) is the word-count table with \(n\) documents and \(p\) words, \(K \ll p\) means far fewer topics than words, and the LSA formulas say “split the table into three smaller tables and keep the first \(K\) patterns, giving each document’s topic scores (\(F\)) and each word’s topic weights (\(L\))”.

In plain words: topics are groups of words that travel together, and each document blends a few of them; LSA finds them by squeezing the count table (scores can be any number), LDA by a chance story worked backwards (scores are shares adding to 100%). The official marking guideline asks only for: “In NLP topics are basically clusters of words. Articles/documents are assumed to consist of different topics, with different weights. SVD for matrix decomp and LDA for probabilities.”

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Naming the methods without the intuition: topics = clusters of words, documents = mixtures of topics.
  • Calling LSA probabilistic. Its numbers are scores, not chances.
  • Saying LDA needs labelled data. It does not; it is unsupervised.
  • Mixing up α (topics per document) and β (main words per topic).
  • Forgetting that K must be chosen by you, and that LDA results depend on the seed.
  • Giving a difference but no similarity (the question asks for both).

Key terms to use

  • topic: cluster of words that appear together
  • mixture of topics: each document blends several themes
  • latent: hidden, never observed directly
  • unsupervised: no answers or labels needed
  • dimension reduction: many words summarised by few topics
  • LSA / SVD: topic model built on table maths
  • factors and loadings: document scores and word weights
  • LDA: topic model built on chances
  • generative model: a story of how words were written
  • Dirichlet prior / sparsity: few topics per document, few main words
  • K: the number of topics you choose
  • random seed: starting point of LDA’s random guessing

True/False traps

  • “LSA is a probabilistic generative model.” → False. Why: LSA is table maths with no chance-based story of how words get written.
  • “LSA is not a probabilistic model and is not seen as a plausible generative model for text.” → True. Why: the lecture says exactly this.
  • “In LDA each document’s topic weights sum to one.” → True. Why: a document’s topic mix is a set of shares, like 70% sport + 30% money.
  • “Lower α in LDA gives each document fewer dominant topics.” → True. Why: α is the dial for how spread out a document’s topics are.
  • “In LDA, a higher α gives each document fewer high-probability topics.” → False. Why: the other way round: a higher α spreads each document over more topics.
  • “LDA always gives the same topics regardless of the random seed.” → False. Why: a different starting point can give different topics.
  • “Topic models need labelled data.” → False. Why: they are unsupervised; nobody gives them the themes.

11. Language models from counting: n-grams

On the exam: not asked in 2025. Likely as True/False (bigrams, smoothing, temperature, loss, perplexity). It also explains why LLM training is “self-supervised”, which was 2025 True/False (f).

The point of it

A language model guesses the next word, like your phone keyboard offering “tomorrow” after you type “see you”. This section builds the simplest one there is: count which word tends to follow which, and guess from those counts.

How it works, in plain words

A language model is a machine that guesses what comes next in a text. Your phone’s autocomplete is a small one. A very big one, like ChatGPT, is an LLM (large language model). Guessing the next piece of text from the pieces before it is called next-token prediction. A token is one small piece of text: a whole word, part of a word, or a single letter. Big chatbots use parts of words. A small model can use single letters, which are called characters. Running example: say you want a computer to invent new first names that sound real. It reads a long list of real names, letter by letter, and learns which letter tends to follow which. Its input is the letters so far; its output is a guess for the next letter. (This names example is the one the lectures use, so it is safe to mention in the exam.)

Simpler text methods treat a text as a loose bag of words: count each word, throw the order away. In the lecturer’s words: “Up to now, we have treated tokens in a text as independent entities.” (Independent = each word has nothing to do with the one before.) A language model drops that idea: the whole point is what comes after what.

Step 1: collect example text, the corpus: a long list of names, or years of a shop’s customer emails.

Step 2: write down every pair of neighbours. Two pieces right next to each other form a bigram (“bi” = two). Pretend “start” and “end” marks around each text let the model learn how texts begin and finish.

Step 3: count, and turn counts into chances. For each piece, work out how often each next piece followed it. That gives a probability: a chance between 0 (never) and 1 (certain). A chance given something you already know (“the chance of ‘you’, given the last word was ‘thank’”) is a conditional probability.

More generally, an n-gram model guesses the next token from the n − 1 tokens before it. A bigram looks back one token. A trigram looks back two. The words a model looks at before guessing are the words it conditions on. So a bigram “conditions on” the one token before, never two. Exam statements use this phrase a lot, so read it as “looks at before guessing”.

Step 4: fix the zeros. A pair you never saw gets chance zero, so the model would call it impossible. That is too harsh, so you pretend every pair was seen one extra time. This is smoothing, and this version is add-one smoothing, also called Laplace smoothing. Think of giving every new restaurant one free review, so none is marked “impossible”.

Step 5: write new text. From “start”, roll a loaded dice that follows the chances, write down what comes up, and roll again until you hit “end”. Picking the next piece at random, weighted by the chances, is sampling. Always taking the favourite instead is greedy decoding, and it writes the same thing every time. A model that always gives the same output for the same start is deterministic. We don’t want that here: a names generator that writes the same name every time is useless.

Step 6: grade the model. Walk through real text and, at each step, look at the chance the model gave to the piece that really came next.

  • The loss is a surprise score. If the model gave the real next word a high chance, it isn’t surprised: low loss. If it gave it a low chance, it is very surprised: high loss. If it gave the real next word a chance of zero, the surprise has no limit, and the loss becomes infinite. A single never-seen pair is enough, which is why Step 4’s smoothing matters. Averaged over the text; lower is better. Its official name is negative log-likelihood. Training means making this score small.
  • Perplexity turns the loss into an easier reading: roughly how many options the model is torn between at each step. A model that knows nothing is torn between all options equally: this blind guesser is the baseline, the yardstick to beat. A good model is torn between only a few. Lower is better, and perplexity is also used to grade big chatbots.

The point of temperature: it’s a creativity dial. When an AI like ChatGPT writes, it picks one word at a time, and for each next word it has given every possible word a chance. After “I walked my…” the chances might be dog 70%, cat 20%, banana 10% (made-up numbers). Temperature changes those chances right before the word is picked. Low temperature (below 1): the favourite gets even more likely, so “dog” almost every time. Safe and predictable, but repetitive. Good for a customer-service bot. Turned all the way down to 0, it becomes greedy decoding: the model always takes its favourite, so you get the same answer every time you ask. Temperature 0 suits jobs with one right answer, such as pulling facts out of a text or sorting texts into boxes. High temperature (above 1): the chances get flatter, so the underdogs, like “cat” and even “banana”, come up more. In the names generator, that means rare letters (characters) get picked more often. Varied and creative, but more mistakes. Good for brainstorming. It doesn’t change what the model has learned; it only changes how the next word is picked.

Where the right answers come from. Machine learning comes in three kinds, depending on who supplies the right answers. Supervised: a human gives them, like a teacher marking every email “spam” or “not spam”. Unsupervised: there are no right answers at all, only patterns to discover, like sorting a pile of news stories into themes nobody named. Self-supervised: the data supplies its own answers. That is the case here. Nobody labels anything: the real next word is already in the text, so the model can check every guess against it. Big chatbots are trained the same way, so the right word for their training is self-supervised, not unsupervised. (Exam papers often say a model is estimated; that means trained.)

Why it works: the reasoning

Language has order. What comes next depends on what came before: after “thank” comes “you” far more often than “banana”. So if a pair showed up often in the past, it is a good bet it will show up again. Counting is the learning.

Smoothing rests on a sensible idea: not seeing something in a limited sample doesn’t prove it can never happen. The loss rests on another: a good model should not be surprised by real text.

The big message is that the chatbots run on this same “guess the next piece” recipe. In the lecturer’s words: “Modern LLMs follow the same recipe, with a transformer and hundreds of thousands of tokens of context.” (A transformer is the network design behind today’s chatbots.)

When to use it, and when not

  • Use it when you need a simple, see-through next-word guesser for short patterns, such as autocomplete in a search box or a form. It is easy to build, and you can check every chance by hand. It is also a fair baseline to compare bigger models against.
  • Don’t use it when the meaning depends on words further back. A bigram forgets everything but the last word, and looking further back makes the count table explode: every extra word of memory multiplies its size again.
  • Compared with bag-of-words: bag-of-words ignores order, which is fine for “what is this text about?”. An n-gram keeps short-range order, which is what you need for “what comes next?”.
  • Compared with a neural network doing the same job: a neural network is a machine of many small dials that learns by nudging them after every wrong guess, instead of counting. Picture building one for exactly the same job as the names generator: guess the next letter from the one letter before. That is the neural network bigram. After training it scores almost exactly the same as the counting bigram, and its learned dials turn into almost the same table of chances. So it is not “much better”. In the lecturer’s words, the network “learns the same thing as counting, but it scales: we can give it more context without the table exploding.” A bigger network that looks back five letters does clearly better. Giving the model more to look back on is what really improves it.

What the exam asks

“What is an n-gram language model?”

Model answer

An n-gram model “predicts the next token by calculating the most likely token based on the previous n−1 tokens”. Next-token prediction means “predicting the next word or token in a sequence given the previous tokens.”

In plain words: it guesses the next piece of text from the few pieces right before it. A bigram looks at one, a trigram at two.

“Why do we need smoothing?”

Model answer

“Without smoothing, any bigram that never appears in the data gets probability 0. Then \(\log(0) = -\infty\), and the loss for a single unseen name like ‘jq’ becomes infinite. Adding a small ‘fake’ count to every bigram (add-one or Laplace smoothing) avoids this.”

The formula \(\log(0) = -\infty\) says: if the model calls the real next piece impossible, its surprise score has no limit.

In plain words: a pair the model never saw gets chance zero, and one such pair makes the whole surprise score endless. Pretending every pair was seen once more fixes that.

“What are loss and perplexity?”

Model answer

“A loss function is a mathematical function used in machine learning and statistics to measure how well a model’s predictions match the actual data. For many probabilistic models, especially those used in tasks like language modeling, the loss function is derived from the negative log likelihood (also called the negative log-likelihood loss).” “Perplexity \(= e^{\text{Loss}}\): roughly how many characters the model is ‘choosing between’ at each step.” “Perplexity is also a standard way to evaluate large language models.”

The formula Perplexity \(= e^{\text{Loss}}\) says: perplexity is the loss converted into “number of options the model is torn between”.

In plain words: the loss is how surprised the model is by the real next piece, and perplexity is how many options it is torn between. For both, lower is better.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Saying a bigram uses the two previous pieces. It uses one; an n-gram uses n − 1.
  • Saying the n-gram model treats words as independent. It does the opposite: it uses the word before.
  • Getting temperature backwards. Higher = more random; lower = safer and more repetitive.
  • Saying temperature changes what the model learned. It only changes how the next word is picked.
  • Saying a high loss or high perplexity is good. Lower is better for both.
  • Saying the neural network version is “much better” than counting. It learns the same thing; its advantage is that it can look further back.
  • Calling LLM training “unsupervised”. The right word is self-supervised.

Key terms to use

  • language model: guesses what comes next in text
  • next-token prediction: guessing the next piece of text
  • token: a word, word piece or letter
  • bigram: two neighbours; looks back one
  • n-gram: guesses from the previous n − 1 pieces
  • add-one (Laplace) smoothing: pretend every pair appeared once more
  • sampling: picking the next piece by weighted dice
  • greedy decoding: always pick the most likely piece
  • temperature: creativity dial: safe below 1, adventurous above
  • loss (negative log-likelihood): average surprise score; lower is better
  • perplexity: how many options it’s torn between
  • self-supervised: the text supplies its own answers

True/False traps

  • “A bigram conditions on the previous two tokens.” → False. Why: as explained in Step 3, “conditions on” means “looks at before guessing”, and a bigram looks back at only one token; it is the trigram that looks back two.
  • “The n-gram model treats tokens as independent.” → False. Why: treating words as independent is the bag-of-words idea, and the n-gram model drops it on purpose: it guesses each word from the word or words just before it.
  • “Higher temperature makes output more predictable.” → False. Why: temperature is the creativity dial, and turning it up flattens the chances, so the output gets more random and less predictable.
  • “Setting the temperature above 1 makes rare characters more likely.” → True. Why: above 1 the favourite loses some of its lead, so the underdogs, here the rare letters in the names generator, get picked more often.
  • “Without smoothing, a single unseen bigram can make the loss infinite.” → True. Why: a pair never seen in the data gets chance zero, and when the model calls the real next piece impossible, its surprise score (the loss) has no limit.
  • “The neural network bigram is much better than the count-based bigram.” → False. Why: the neural network bigram does the same job as counting and scores almost exactly the same; its only advantage is that it can grow to look further back without the table exploding.
  • “Large Language Models are estimated using unsupervised learning.” (2025, 1(f)) → False. Why: “estimated” means trained, and the training is self-supervised, not unsupervised: the right answer (the real next word) is already in the text, so there are right answers, they just don’t come from a human.

12. Neural networks, only what the exam needs

On the exam: the one-hot vector was asked in 2025, Q3(c) (2.5 points). Softmax, loss and the training words come back in the word-embedding and LLM questions, and make easy True/False items.

The point of it

A machine that learns a pattern from examples on its own, by turning many small dials, instead of someone writing the rules or counting every possible combination. word2vec (word embeddings) and the big chatbots are built from exactly these parts.

How it works, in plain words

A box of many small dials. Picture a sound mixing desk covered in knobs. Numbers go in at one end and come out at the other. Each knob decides how much one incoming number counts: turn it up and that number matters more. Such a knob is a weight. One extra knob adds a fixed amount whatever comes in, like a shop adding the same delivery fee to every order: the bias. All the weights and biases together are the parameters, everything the network learns.

Small calculators, stacked in layers. The box is made of tiny calculators called neurons. Each one multiplies its inputs by their weights, adds it all up plus the bias, and pushes the total through a squashing rule (the activation function) to keep it in a useful range. A layer is a group of neurons side by side; one layer’s outputs are the next layer’s inputs.

  • The input layer is where numbers come in. It does no calculation, so it isn’t counted.
  • Hidden layers sit in the middle. You never see their numbers directly.
  • The output layer gives the final answer.

When people count a network’s layers, they leave the input layer out, because it only passes numbers in. So a network with one hidden layer is called a two-layer network: the hidden layer plus the output layer. Think of a house: you don’t count the front door as a room. Many hidden layers stacked up is called deep learning.

Words must become numbers first. The network only handles numbers. So each word becomes a one-hot vector. A vector is a list of numbers. A one-hot vector is a list as long as the vocabulary (the full list of words the model knows), all zeros except a single 1 in that word’s own slot. Think of a row of light switches where only one is on: the switch for “dog”.

All the weights of one layer, written out as a table with a row or column per word, form a weight matrix (a matrix is a table of numbers, like a spreadsheet). In the first layer, the one-hot vector is multiplied by this table. The zeros wipe out every other word’s weights, and only that word’s own column of weights comes through. You do not get all the columns added up; you get one. So the one-hot vector works as a selection vector, like a finger pointing at one name in a phone book.

This is exactly how word2vec starts. word2vec is a small network that learns a short list of numbers for every word, called the word’s embedding, so that words with similar meanings get similar lists. Those lists sit in the first layer’s table, one column per word. The one-hot vector picks out the column of the word being fed in, and learning good columns is the whole point of word2vec.

From scores to chances: softmax. Say the job is to guess the next word. The output layer gives one raw score for each possible word, of any size, even negative. Softmax turns scores into chances that add up to 100%. Chances are often written between 0 and 1, so “add up to 100%” is the same as “sum to 1”. The biggest score gets the biggest chance, but every option keeps some.

How training nudges the weights. At first the weights are random, so the guesses are bad. Then:

  1. The network makes a guess with its current weights.
  2. A loss (also cost function) measures how wrong it was in one number: a high chance on the right answer gives a small loss, a low chance a big one. Its name is negative log-likelihood or cross-entropy.
  3. Backpropagation traces the error backwards through the layers and works out, for every weight, which way to turn it so the loss would drop.
  4. Gradient descent turns every weight a small step that way, like feeling your way down a foggy hill.
  5. Repeat many times. One full pass through all the examples is an epoch.

Doing each step on a small random handful of examples (a mini-batch) instead of all of them is stochastic gradient descent (SGD); “stochastic” means random. It is faster, and a bit noisier.

Why networks took off. A network can learn its training examples by heart instead of the general pattern, like memorising last year’s exam answers without understanding them. This is overfitting. Tricks against it are called regularisation. One such trick is dropout: during training, switch off some random neurons each time, so the network can’t lean on any single one. (How dropout works is [Not in course files — general knowledge].) Networks also need huge numbers of small multiplications, and a GPU (a graphics chip) can do thousands of them at the same time. Networks are not new; they have a long history. They became popular partly thanks to these newer developments: SGD, dropout and regularisation, and GPUs.

Why it works: the reasoning

Nobody has to name the patterns. The hidden layers build their own useful summaries of the input, called latent features (“latent” = hidden). Thousands of small nudges, each making the error a bit smaller, add up to a good model.

An older method, logistic regression, is a classifier: something that sorts items into groups, such as “spam” or “not spam”. It takes a weighted sum of the inputs and squeezes it into a chance. A network whose last layer is softmax does the same thing at the end, so that last layer works like logistic regression. And since every layer does a weighted sum and a squashing step, a deep network is like layer after layer of logistic regressions, small judges each passing their verdict on to the next. The difference: in logistic regression a human picks the inputs; in a network, the earlier layers build them.

When to use it, and when not

  • Use it when the patterns are too many to write as rules or to count (spotting spam, guessing the next word from a long stretch of text) and you have many examples.
  • Don’t use it when you have few examples: the network can learn them by heart instead of the pattern (overfitting, like memorising last year’s exam answers). It also needs a lot of computing power.
  • Compared with counting (n-grams): a count table explodes every time you look one more word back; a network takes in more context without exploding. On the same short context, it learns the same thing as counting.

More ideas the exam can test

The neural network bigram: the simplest network, with no hidden layer. A bigram is a pair of neighbours, and a bigram model guesses the next piece of text from the one piece before it. Say you want a computer to invent new first names: it reads real names letter by letter and guesses each next letter from the letter before. A neural network can do this job. Each letter goes in as a one-hot vector, a single table of dials turns it into one score per possible next letter, and softmax turns those scores into chances. That is all: the input goes straight to the output layer, so this network has no hidden layer. After training it guesses almost exactly as well as plain counting of letter pairs. A network with at least one hidden layer is called an MLP; a bigger one that looks back five letters does better. This is the example the lectures use, so “the neural network bigram” in an exam statement means this network.

What the exam asks

2025, Q3(c) (2.5 points): “What is a one-hot-vector? Describe its usage in the first layer of the word2vec model.”

Model answer

A one-hot vector represents a word as a vector of length \(p\) (the vocabulary size) with a 1 in the position of that word and 0 everywhere else. It is a way to feed words, which computers cannot use directly, into a neural network. In the first layer of word2vec, \(h^1 = W^1 x\), where \(W^1\) is the \(K\times p\) target-embedding matrix. Multiplying \(W^1\) by the one-hot vector just selects the column of \(W^1\) that belongs to the target word, so the one-hot vector works as a selection (look-up) vector. That selected column is the word’s \(K\)-dimensional embedding (typically \(K=300\)), which is learned during training and is the “ultimate goal” of the model.

The formula \(h^1 = W^1 x\) says: the first layer’s output is the weight table (\(K\) rows, one column per word) multiplied by the one-hot vector, which picks out that word’s column.

In plain words: a one-hot vector is a “which word?” marker, all zeros and one 1. In word2vec’s first step it picks one column out of a big table, and that column is the word’s list of numbers, which is what word2vec is there to learn.

“How is a neural network trained, and why did neural networks become popular?”

Model answer

With the current weights the network makes a prediction, and a cost function (e.g. negative log-likelihood) measures how far it is from the truth. Backpropagation computes how the cost changes with each weight, and gradient descent moves every weight a small step in the direction that lowers the cost. This is repeated many times over the data. Neural networks became popular partly thanks to stochastic gradient descent, dropout and regularisation (better learning, less overfitting), and GPU computing.

In plain words: guess, score how wrong the guess was, work out which way each dial should turn, turn them all a small step, repeat. They took off thanks to faster learning, tricks against memorising, and graphics chips.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Saying a one-hot vector “counts how often a word appears”. It only marks which word it is.
  • Saying softmax picks the single biggest value. It gives every option a chance, and the chances add up to 100%.
  • Mixing up backpropagation and gradient descent. Backpropagation finds the directions; gradient descent turns the weights.
  • Saying neural networks are popular because they are “new”. They have a long history; newer developments made them popular.

Key terms to use

  • weights: dials saying how much each input counts
  • hidden layer: middle layer you never see directly
  • one-hot vector: all zeros, one 1 for the word
  • selection vector: one-hot used to pick one column
  • softmax: turns scores into chances adding to 100%
  • loss / cost function: one “how wrong” score to shrink
  • backpropagation: works out which way each weight goes
  • gradient descent: turn weights a small step downhill
  • overfitting: memorising examples, not learning the pattern

True/False traps

  • “Multiplying a one-hot vector by a weight matrix returns the sum of all rows of the matrix.” → False. Why: the one-hot vector is all zeros except one 1, and every zero wipes out its part of the weight table, so you get only the one row or column where the 1 sits, not everything added up.
  • “Softmax outputs always sum to 1.” → True. Why: softmax turns raw scores into chances that always add up to 100%, and 100% written as a number between 0 and 1 is 1.
  • “A softmax output layer makes a neural network like logistic regression.” → True. Why: logistic regression turns a weighted sum into a chance, and a network ending in softmax does the same thing in its last layer.
  • “A deep neural network can be seen as layer after layer of logistic regression classifiers.” → True. Why: every layer does a weighted sum and a squashing step, which is what logistic regression does, and passes its verdict on to the next layer.
  • “The input layer is counted when we call a network with one hidden layer a two-layer network.” → False. Why: the input layer only passes numbers in and does no calculation, so it is left out; the two layers are the hidden layer and the output layer.
  • “The neural network bigram has a hidden layer.” → False. Why: the neural network bigram, which guesses the next letter from the one before, goes straight from input to output through a single table of dials, so it has no hidden layer.

13. Word embeddings: word meanings as positions on a map

On the exam: a whole 15-point block in 2025: Q3(a) the assumption and the PMI matrix (5 points), Q3(b) word2vec vs BERT (2.5 points), Q3(c) the one-hot vector in word2vec (2.5 points), Q3(d) a cosine-similarity drawing (5 points), plus True/False (d) on SVD and the PMI matrix. Also useful in the reflection, for measuring how strongly a text links two ideas.

The point of it

Give every word a spot on a map of meaning, so a computer knows that “car” and “automobile” belong together, without anyone writing a dictionary for it.

How it works, in plain words

The problem. The usual word-count table has one row per document, one column per word, each cell a count. This is bag-of-words, and the table is the document-term matrix (a matrix is a table of numbers, like a spreadsheet). In it, “car” and “automobile” are separate columns with nothing linking them, and the words around each word are ignored.

A map of meaning. Imagine a map where every word has a spot, and words with similar meanings sit close together. A spot is a short list of numbers, like map coordinates. A list of numbers is a vector, drawn as an arrow from the centre of the map to the spot. Word maps use around 100 or 300 numbers per word instead of a paper map’s two. A word’s spot is its word embedding. The slides’ slogan: “embedding is location”.

The clue: the company a word keeps. “Apricot” and “pineapple” both turn up near “sugar”. “Digital” and “information” both turn up near “computer” and “data”. The word we look at is the target word; the words near it, inside a window of a few words to each side, are its context words. Two words in the same window co-occur.

There are two ways to turn “who appears with whom” into map spots.

Way 1: count, re-weight, squeeze.

  1. Count. Build a table with one row and one column per word. Each cell says how often the two words appeared in the same window. This is the co-occurrence matrix. It is words × words, not documents × words.
  2. Re-weight with PMI. Common words like “the” sit next to everything, so raw counts make them everybody’s neighbour. The fairer question: do these two words meet more often than pure luck would give, given how common each is? Two people who both visit a café daily bump into each other by luck. Two who rarely go, yet keep turning up together: that means something. The score for “how much more than luck” is PMI (pointwise mutual information). Positive means the words attract each other. Negative scores are hard to trust, so they are set to 0: that version is PPMI (positive PMI).
  3. Squeeze with SVD. The PMI table is huge and mostly zeros, which is called sparse. A tool called SVD (singular value decomposition) keeps only the strongest few patterns and gives each word a short list of numbers, most of them not zero, which is called dense. Think of summing up a long survey by a few main themes, with each person scored on each theme. Keeping only the strongest patterns is truncated SVD. The same tool appears in the topic-model method LSA, but there it runs on the document-term table; here, in the slides’ words, “not on C, but on M!” (\(C\) is the document-term table, \(M\) the PMI table).

Way 2: a guessing game (word2vec). word2vec is a small neural network: a machine of many small dials that learns by nudging them after every wrong guess. It plays a guessing game with real text, in two versions:

  • Skipgram: see one word, guess the words around it. “Here’s ‘cat’. Which words would you expect nearby?”
  • CBOW: see the words around, guess the word in the middle. A fill-in-the-blank exercise. (CBOW stands for “continuous bag of words” [Not in course files — general knowledge].)

The text supplies the right answers (the real neighbours), so no human labels anything. This is self-supervised learning.

One-hot vectors in the first layer. The network only handles numbers, so each word enters as a one-hot vector: a list as long as the vocabulary (the full word list), all zeros except a 1 in that word’s own slot, like a row of light switches with one turned on. The first layer holds a big table of dials, the target embedding matrix, with one column per word. Multiplying the one-hot vector by this table wipes out every column except that word’s own. So the one-hot vector works as a selection vector. After training, each word’s column is its spot on the map. Learning that table is the whole point of word2vec. At the end, a step called softmax turns the network’s scores into chances that add up to 100%: the chance that each word is a neighbour.

Comparing two words. Similar words have arrows pointing in nearly the same direction. The score for this is cosine similarity: it looks at the angle between two arrows, not their length. A small angle means similar; a right angle means unrelated.

Directions carry meaning. You can add and subtract word arrows. The famous example: King − Man + Woman ≈ Queen. The step from “man” to “king” is a “royalty” direction; add it to “woman” and you land near “queen”.

One spot per word, or a new spot per sentence. word2vec gives every word one fixed spot, whatever the sentence. These embeddings are static. Newer models such as BERT, built on the transformer (the network design behind today’s chatbots), give a word a new spot in each sentence, depending on the words around it. These are contextual (context-specific) embeddings.

Why it works: the reasoning

The assumption is that words used in similar surroundings have similar meanings. The linguist Firth (1957): “You shall know a word by the company it keeps!” This is usually called the distributional hypothesis; the slides quote Firth without using that name [Not in course files — general knowledge]. Think of guessing a stranger’s hobby from their friends: someone who always hangs around climbers and talks about ropes is probably a climber.

Each step follows from that. PMI keeps the meetings that say something beyond luck. SVD works because words with similar company have similar rows in the table, so after squeezing they get similar short lists. word2vec works because, to guess neighbours well, words with similar neighbours must get similar spots. Contextual embeddings exist because one word can mean different things: “apple” the fruit and “Apple” the company keep different company.

When to use it, and when not

  • Use it when you need similarity of meaning: finding “car” pages when someone searches “automobile”, or tracking how strongly two ideas are linked over time. Course examples: how strongly the news links recession and risk with credit, month by month (Labonne & Thorsrud 2023); a transition-risk index for Norway that moves with climate-policy events (Kapfhammer, Larsen & Thorsrud 2020); how the meaning of social class shifted over the 20th century (Kozlowski et al. 2019).
  • Don’t rely on static embeddings when a word has several meanings that matter for your question: word2vec mixes “apple” the fruit and “Apple” the company into one spot. Use contextual embeddings (BERT) instead.
  • Watch rare words. PMI gives rare word pairs blown-up scores: one lucky meeting looks hugely “more than chance”. The fix is add-one smoothing: add the same pretend count to every cell before calculating.
  • Compared with bag-of-words: bag-of-words counts words per document and knows nothing about which words mean similar things. Embeddings learn that from context.
  • Counting + SVD vs word2vec: same goal, two routes. The slides warn that “Neural Network-based methods might not outperform the simple SVD” (Levy et al. 2015).

What the exam asks

2025, Q3(a) (5 points): “What is the underlying assumption of word-embedding-based methods, and what is the PMI matrix?”

Model answer

Word-embedding methods rest on the distributional hypothesis: “You shall know a word by the company it keeps” (Firth). Words that occur in similar contexts have similar meanings, so a word can be represented by a dense vector learned from its co-occurrence with other words. Similar words then end up close to each other in the vector space. Unlike bag-of-words, context is the information used.

The PMI (pointwise mutual information) matrix is a \(p\times p\) matrix with one row per word and one column per context word. Element \((i,j)\) measures how much more often word \(i\) appears within a window of \(b\) words of word \(j\) than we would expect if the two were independent: \(M_{ij}=\log_2\big(p_{ij}/(p_ip_j)\big)\), where \(p_{ij}\) is the joint co-occurrence probability and \(p_i, p_j\) are the individual probabilities. Positive values mean the words attract each other (e.g. information–data = 0.57 in the lecture example). Negative values are usually set to zero (PPMI), and add-one smoothing is used because PMI gives rare words high weight. The PMI matrix is large and sparse, so word embeddings are obtained by applying truncated SVD to it and using the rows of \(U\).

The formula \(M_{ij}=\log_2\big(p_{ij}/(p_ip_j)\big)\) says: compare how often two words really meet with how often they would meet if they were unrelated; more than luck gives a positive score. (\(p\times p\) means one row and one column per vocabulary word; \(U\) is the table SVD hands back, with one row per word.)

In plain words: words that keep similar company mean similar things. The PMI table scores every pair of words by how much more often they meet than luck would give, and squeezing it with SVD gives each word its short list of coordinates.

2025, Q3(b) (2.5 points): “Describe in max two sentences the biggest conceptual difference between word embeddings estimated using, e.g., the word2vec model and those obtained from, e.g., BERT.”

Model answer

A word2vec embedding is static: each word gets one fixed vector no matter which sentence it is used in, so “apple” the fruit and “Apple” the computer company share the same vector. BERT produces contextual embeddings: the Transformer’s self-attention lets the vector for a word depend on the surrounding words, so “bank” next to “water” and “bank” next to “credit” get different vectors.

In plain words: word2vec gives a word one spot forever; BERT looks at the sentence first and gives the word a spot that fits it. (Self-attention is the part of the transformer where each word looks at the other words in the sentence.)

Key terms to use

  • distributional hypothesis: similar company means similar meaning
  • Firth (1957): “know a word by its company”
  • word embedding: a word’s spot on the meaning map
  • co-occurrence: two words appearing near each other
  • PMI: how much more than luck two words meet
  • PPMI: PMI with negative scores set to 0
  • large and sparse: huge table, mostly zeros
  • truncated SVD: squeeze the table to its strongest patterns
  • dense vector: short list, few zeros
  • Skipgram / CBOW: guess neighbours from word / word from neighbours
  • one-hot / selection vector: all zeros, one 1; picks a column
  • target embedding matrix: table holding each word’s map spot
  • cosine similarity: angle between two word arrows
  • static vs contextual: one fixed spot vs a new spot per sentence

True/False traps

  • “To obtain word embeddings we can apply the Singular Value Decomposition to the PMI matrix.” (2025, 1(d)) → True. Why: the slides say the PMI matrix is large and sparse, so use SVD “but now not on C, but on M!”; each word’s row in the result is its embedding.
  • “Skipgram predicts a word from its surrounding words.” → False. Why: that is CBOW; Skipgram sees the word and guesses its neighbours.
  • “PMI gives rare word pairs low weight, so add-one smoothing is not needed.” → False. Why: it is the opposite: PMI gives rare words high weight, which is why smoothing is used.
  • “In word2vec, the word ‘bank’ gets the same embedding in ‘river bank’ and ‘bank loan’.” → True. Why: word2vec gives each word one fixed spot whatever the sentence.
  • “BERT gives each word one fixed embedding.” → False. Why: BERT’s embeddings are contextual: a new spot in each sentence.
  • “Neural-network embeddings always outperform SVD on the PMI matrix.” → False. Why: the slides say they “might not outperform the simple SVD”.
  • “Word embeddings are learned from human-made labels.” → False. Why: the text supplies its own answers (the real neighbours).

Part 4: Large Language Models (exam Block 4)

14. What a Large Language Model is, and how it is trained

On the exam: asked in 2025 as True/False (b) “Hugging Face” and (f) “unsupervised learning”, 2 points each. It is the base for the whole 25-point Transformers & LLMs block, and useful for the reflection (where an LLM fits in a business).

The point of it

An LLM is your phone’s next-word suggestion, made enormous. How it is trained tells you what it knows, what it cannot remember, and how you can change what it does.

How it works, in plain words

A next-word guesser. A Large Language Model (LLM) is a program that has read a huge amount of text and learned which bits of text tend to come next. ChatGPT is a product built on one [Not in course files — general knowledge]. You give it some text. It guesses the next small piece, adds it to the end, and guesses again, until the answer is finished. Each piece is a token: a whole word or a part of a word (Section 15). The guess is never one certain word. The model gives every possible next token a chance, called a probability, from 0% (impossible) to 100% (certain).

Billions of knobs. Picture a giant mixing desk with billions of tiny volume knobs. Each knob is one number, called a weight or a parameter (the two words mean the same here). Where the knobs sit decides which guesses come out. The blueprint that arranges the knobs is the Transformer (Section 16). A standard definition worth learning: an LLM is “A learned set of parameters (weights), used with a tokenizer and inference software to generate language.” In plain words: a big pile of learned numbers, a tool that cuts text into pieces, and a program that runs the numbers.

Training: guess, measure how wrong, nudge. At the start the knobs are set at random, so the guesses are rubbish. Training means setting the knobs well by showing the model examples. It is a loop:

  1. Show the model some text with the next token hidden.
  2. Let it guess.
  3. Reveal the real token and measure how wrong the guess was. This “how wrong” score is the loss, like points lost on a test. For LLMs it is called cross-entropy loss (explained under “More ideas the exam can test” below).
  4. Nudge every knob a tiny bit, so the real token gets a little more likely next time. The nudging rule is gradient descent, and the trick that finds which way to turn each knob is backpropagation (both explained below).
  5. Repeat, billions of times.

The three training stages, and why each exists. They are “common ingredients, not a fixed pipeline”: not every model uses every stage.

  1. Pre-training: “Learn to predict tokens from large collections of text, code, and, for some models, other media.” This is the guessing game above, over books, websites and code. Why it exists: this is where the model picks up language, facts and style. It can use almost unlimited text, because nobody has to label anything. Side effect: it “can also memorize examples and errors” (memorization: storing exact bits of its training text, mistakes included, and repeating them later). Some models add an extra round on chosen text afterwards, called mid-training.
  2. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT): “Learn from examples of instructions and desired responses.” People write example questions with good answers, and the model learns from them, like a new employee studying model answers. Why it exists: after pre-training the model is good at continuing text, but not at following instructions. SFT teaches it to answer. This is the supervised stage, because people wrote the right answers. (Fine-tuning in general means extra training of an already-trained model. It changes the weights.)
  3. Preference training (its full name is “preference optimization / reinforcement learning”): “Improve responses using preferences or rewards, including verifiable results such as passing code tests.” The model is shown pairs of answers and which one people preferred, or it gets a reward (a score) for good answers, like a dog getting a treat for sitting. Why it exists: it pushes the model towards the kind of answers people actually want.

Everything after pre-training is called post-training. It “develops instruction following, tool use, and reasoning capabilities.”

Answer time: the knobs are frozen. Using the finished model to write answers is called inference. During inference the weights do not move at all. So the model does not learn from your chat.

The context window: the desk. While answering, the model can only use the text in front of it right now. That text is its context. The most it can see at once, counted in tokens, is its context window: “from a few thousand to about one million tokens, depending on the model”. Think of a desk: only the papers on the desk can be read. The desk holds the app’s hidden instructions, the chat so far, any uploaded documents, and the answer being written. “Everything the model uses at answer time must fit in the window; nothing else is ‘remembered’ unless the application re-inserts it.” A bigger window costs more, is slower, and “does not guarantee that every detail is used reliably”.

So there are two very different things you can change: what is on the desk (the context), and the gut feeling (the weights). The key line to remember: “Uploading a document changes the context. Fine-tuning changes the weights.” RAG (retrieval-augmented generation: search your own documents and paste the relevant bits into the context) is uploading done automatically. “Retrieval changes context; fine-tuning changes weights. Neither guarantees accuracy.”

Open weights and Hugging Face. Some makers publish their learned numbers so anyone can download the model and run it on their own machines. This is called open weights. Put formally: “downloadable model parameters; permissions depend on the license” (a license is the legal terms saying what you may do with it, like the rules in a rental contract). Open weights “do not necessarily include training data or the full training recipe”. Think of being handed a finished cake: you can eat it and share it, but you don’t get the recipe or the list of ingredients. The best-known place to download open-weight models is Hugging Face. Its official description is “the AI community’s open-source hub for Machine Learning and Natural Language Processing”. In plain words: a free website, a bit like an app store, where people share models. That description comes from the course outline, and the 2025 exam tested it. (Open-source: free for anyone to use and share. Natural Language Processing (NLP): computers working with human language.)

Why it works: the reasoning

Text carries its own answer key. Every sentence ever written shows which word came after which. So the model can practise guessing on all of it, with no teacher. This is called self-supervised learning. The standard explanation, worth quoting in the exam: “Training LLMs is often called self-supervised training. Why? We use the actual text and train the model to predict the next word (or masked words). I.e., no need for manually creating training data.” (Masked words: words hidden inside a sentence, fill-in-the-blank style. Some models, such as BERT, practise that way.)

Why is it not “unsupervised”? Compare the three kinds of learning:

  • Supervised: a person wrote the right answer for each example, like emails marked “spam” or “not spam”.
  • Unsupervised: there are no right answers at all; the computer only hunts for patterns, like sorting customers into groups nobody named. Topic models such as LDA work this way.
  • Self-supervised: there is a right answer every time (the real next word), but no person wrote it. The text supplies it.

Think of covering the next word in a book with a sticky note, guessing, then lifting the note to check. Nobody wrote an answer key; the book is the answer key.

Guessing the next word well, at huge scale, forces the model to absorb grammar, facts and style, because all of them help the guess. But it learns what sounds likely, not what has been checked. That is why it can invent facts with total confidence, which is called hallucination.

When to use it, and when not

  • Use it when the task is writing, summarising, translating or answering common questions, because it has seen huge amounts of that kind of text. There are four ways a business can use one: a chat app, an API (a doorway that lets your own program send text and get answers back), an agent (the model loops through a task using outside tools), or running it on your own machines. “The mode determines the cost structure, where data goes, and who is accountable for errors.”
  • Put your facts in the context (upload or RAG) when the model needs your company’s documents for this answer. Fine-tune when you need a lasting change in how it behaves on one task; that needs labelled examples (each example comes with the right answer, written by a person). For instance, to fine-tune a sentiment classifier (a model that sorts texts such as reviews into positive or negative), you need texts already tagged positive or negative. That makes this kind of fine-tuning ordinary supervised learning.
  • Don’t use it when facts must be right and nobody checks (it can hallucinate), when the task depends on single letters or digits (it only sees tokens, so spelling and arithmetic can go wrong), or when the material will not fit in the window.
  • Bigger is not always better. More parameters “can increase capacity, but performance also depends on data quality, training, and the task,” and “the largest model is not always the best choice.” Smaller models can suit narrow tasks or running on your own machine. Think of vehicles: a lorry carries the most, but for a quick trip to the shop a bike is the better choice.
  • Compared with training your own from scratch: “Training a frontier model from scratch is beyond a typical laptop” (a frontier model is one of the biggest of its time). So most users prompt or fine-tune an existing model.

More ideas the exam can test

Mixture of experts (MoE): only part of the model works on each token. The point: it makes a huge model cheaper to run. An MoE model is split into many “expert” parts, and for each token only a few experts are switched on. In exam words: “only a subset of the parameters is activated for each token.” Think of a big hospital: a patient with a broken arm sees two specialists, not all 200 doctors. So the model can be huge in total but does much less work per token. It does NOT mean every knob is used for every token; that is exactly the trap.

Cross-entropy loss: the “how wrong” score. The point: training needs one number that says how bad a guess was, so it knows what to fix. Cross-entropy is the name of that score for LLMs. It looks only at the chance the model gave to the true next token. A high chance on the true token means a small penalty, a low chance means a big penalty, and 100% on the right token means no penalty at all: the loss is zero. Think of a weather forecaster: saying “90% rain” on a day it rains costs almost nothing, saying “5% rain” on that day costs a lot. For a whole sentence, the penalties at each position are averaged. (The formal definition, “the negative log probability the model assigns to the next word”, is the same idea in maths words.)

Gradient descent: walking downhill in fog. The point: it is the rule for nudging the knobs in step 4 of the training loop. Picture yourself on a foggy mountain, trying to reach the valley floor. You can’t see far, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet, so you take a small step downhill, feel again, and step again. Here the height is the loss and your position is the setting of all the knobs. The list of “which way is uphill” for every knob is called the gradient, and “step the other way, again and again” is gradient descent. Formally, it is “a general method to find the optimum (or minimum) of a function”: the lowest point of the loss.

Mini-batch, epoch and online training: how much data per nudge. The point: looking at all the training text before every single nudge would be far too slow. So each nudge uses a small random handful of examples, called a mini-batch (or batch). Think of tasting a spoonful of soup instead of the whole pot. One full trip through all the training data is an epoch, like reading the whole textbook once. The two extremes have their own names. If the handful is the whole data set (the whole pot), that is standard gradient descent. If it is a single example, so there is one nudge after every example, that is online training, which comes “with a lot of noise” (the steps wobble a lot). Nudging with random mini-batches is called stochastic gradient descent (SGD), “stochastic” meaning random; it is the standard way to train large networks. Don’t mix them up: a mini-batch of one is online training, not standard gradient descent, and an epoch is the full pass, not one handful.

Backpropagation and the chain rule: finding which way to turn every knob. The point: an LLM has billions of knobs, and gradient descent needs to know, for each one, whether turning it up makes the loss better or worse. The model’s calculation is a long chain of small steps, so a change in one knob passes along the chain, link by link, to the loss, like a row of dominoes. The chain rule is the maths rule for following an effect through such a chain. Backpropagation starts at the end (the loss) and walks backwards through the chain once, and that one backward sweep gives the direction for every knob at the same time. Think of a factory line that made a faulty product: one walk back from the end, station by station, tells you how much each station added to the fault. The formal statement: backpropagation “is a ML term for reverse-mode differentiation, which in turn is just one way of applying the multivariate chain rule”. (Differentiation: working out how a small change in one thing changes another. Reverse mode: doing it from the end backwards.) So the three names describe the same trick.

Teacher forcing: show the true earlier words during training. The point: when the model practises guessing, say, the fifth word of a sentence, it needs the first four words to work from. Should those be the real words from the text, or its own earlier guesses? Teacher forcing means always giving it the real ones. Formally: “give the model the correct history sequence to predict the next word rather than feeding the model its best case from the previous … time step”. Think of a teacher who corrects each word of your dictation before you write the next one. It does NOT mean feeding the model its own predictions; that happens at answer time, when the model writes on from its own words.

What the exam asks

Why is LLM pre-training called self-supervised, and which training step is supervised? (likely 2–5 sentence question)

Model answer

In pre-training the model reads large amounts of text and, at each position, predicts the next token; the true next token in the text is the target, and future tokens are hidden from the prediction. Because the labels come from the text itself, no one has to label data by hand, so it is called self-supervised rather than supervised or unsupervised. The supervised step comes later: in supervised fine-tuning (SFT) the model learns from examples of instructions paired with desired responses written by people.

In plain words: the text itself shows the right next word, so nobody writes answers by hand: that is self-supervised. Later, people write example questions with good answers, and that step (SFT) is the supervised one.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Calling pre-training “unsupervised”. This was the 2025 trap. The right word is “self-supervised”, because the text supplies the right answers. It is the word the lectures use, so the graders look for it.
  • Saying pre-training uses human-labelled data. It uses plain text with no labels.
  • Saying the model “learns from your chat” or “remembers you”. The weights are fixed; only the context changes.
  • Saying RAG or uploading a document “retrains” the model. It only gives the model more to read.
  • Listing the training stages without saying why each exists, or forgetting to name SFT as the supervised step.
  • Claiming a bigger model or a longer answer is automatically more correct. “A longer answer or a persuasive explanation does not guarantee correctness.”

Key terms to use

  • next-token prediction: guessing the next piece of text
  • parameters / weights: the numbers the model learned
  • self-supervised pre-training: learning from raw text, no human labels
  • supervised fine-tuning (SFT): learning from human-written example answers
  • preference optimization / reinforcement learning: rewarding answers people like better
  • inference with fixed weights: using the model without changing it
  • context window, counted in tokens: how much it reads at once
  • context vs weights: what it reads now vs what it learned
  • open weights: learned numbers anyone can download
  • Hugging Face hub: free website for sharing AI models

True/False traps

  • “Large Language Models are estimated using unsupervised learning” (2025, T/F f) → False. Why: “estimated” means trained. As “Why it works” explains, there is a right answer every time (the real next word), supplied by the text itself, so the right word is self-supervised, not unsupervised.
  • “HuggingFace is a open source hub for Natural Language Processing” (2025, T/F b) → True. Why: Hugging Face is a free website where people share AI models, and its official description is an “open-source hub for Machine Learning and Natural Language Processing”. The small grammar slip (“a open”) does not matter.
  • “RAG updates the model’s weights” → False. Why: RAG searches your documents and pastes the useful bits into what the model reads. It changes the context (the desk), not the weights (what the model learned).
  • “An LLM updates its weights during each conversation” → False. Why: during inference (answer time) the knobs are frozen, so the model does not learn from your chat; only the context changes.
  • “In an MoE model all parameters are used for every token” → False. Why: in a mixture-of-experts model only a few expert parts switch on for each token, like a patient who sees two specialists, not every doctor in the hospital.
  • “Open weights always include the training data” → False. Why: open weights give you the learned numbers to download, not the texts the model learned from: the finished cake, not the recipe or the ingredients.
  • “Bigger is always better” → False. Why: results also depend on data quality, training and the task, and a smaller model can be the better choice for a narrow task or for running on your own machine.
  • “Fine-tuning a sentiment classifier needs labelled data” → True. Why: fine-tuning for a task like this is supervised learning, so the model needs example texts already tagged positive or negative.
  • “The cross-entropy loss is zero when the model gives the true next word probability one.” → True. Why: cross-entropy is the “how wrong” score, and it shrinks as the chance given to the true word grows; if the model was 100% sure of the right word, nothing is lost, so the score is zero.
  • “An epoch is one pass through the full training set” → True. Why: an epoch is one full read-through of all the training data, like reading the whole textbook once.
  • “A mini-batch of size one is standard gradient descent” → False. Why: a mini-batch of one example is online training (one nudge after every example); standard gradient descent uses the whole data set, the whole pot of soup, for each nudge.
  • “Teacher forcing feeds the model its own predictions” → False. Why: teacher forcing means the opposite: during training the model is shown the true earlier words, not its own guesses.
  • “Backpropagation is reverse-mode differentiation / the chain rule” → True. Why: these are three names for the same trick: one backward sweep from the loss, following the chain rule, that works out which way to turn every knob.

15. Tokens: the pieces an LLM actually reads

On the exam: asked in 2025 as Q4(c) (5 points) and True/False (c) (2 points). Useful for the reflection too: prices and length limits are counted in tokens.

The point of it

A computer can only work with numbers, so text must be cut into pieces and each piece numbered. Tokens are the clever middle size: not whole words, not single letters.

How it works, in plain words

A Large Language Model (LLM), the kind of program behind ChatGPT, writes by guessing the next small piece of text, over and over. That piece is a token. The tool that cuts text into tokens is the tokenizer, and the cutting is called tokenization. A token can be a whole word, a part of a word (a subword), a space, a punctuation mark or a chunk of digits.

Common words stay whole, as one token. Rare words are built from a few familiar pieces: “unbelievable” becomes un | believ | able, and “strawberry” becomes st | raw | berry.

The tokenizer has a fixed list of all the pieces it knows. That list is its vocabulary. Each piece has its own ID number, like a barcode on a product (its token ID). The model only ever receives these IDs. Inside the model, each ID is turned into an embedding: a list of numbers (a vector) the model can calculate with, chosen so that pieces with similar meanings get similar lists. A key phrase to remember: “We do not input words per se, but tokens.” (“Per se” means “as such”.)

How the list of pieces is built: Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE). Nobody writes the vocabulary by hand. It is learned from example text, in these steps:

  1. Take a big pile of example text. Cut every word into single letters, with a small tag marking where each word ends.
  2. Count how often each pair of side-by-side pieces appears, for example “t” followed by “h”. Each piece at this moment, a letter or an already-glued chunk, is called a symbol, and two symbols sitting next to each other are adjacent symbols.
  3. Take the pair that appears most often and glue it into one new piece (a merge). Do this everywhere in the text, and add the new piece to the vocabulary.
  4. Go back to step 2. Stop when the vocabulary has the size you want.

It is like noticing you type “th” all the time and adding one key for it. To cut up a new sentence later, the tokenizer starts from its letters and repeats the same merges, in the same order.

Tokens are not words. Put formally: “Token counts depend on language and formatting; one word need not equal one token.” Say you take a short English sentence, translate it into Norwegian, and count the tokens of both. The Norwegian version needs more, because its words get chopped into more pieces. This is the example the lectures used, so it is a safe one to write in the exam.

Why it works: the reasoning

There are two bad extremes:

  • One number per whole word. The list never ends: every name, typo, plural and new product name needs its own number. A word not seen in training is unknown to the model.
  • One number per letter. The list is tiny, but every sentence becomes very long and slow to process, and each piece carries little meaning.

Tokens are the middle way. The key sentence: “Subword methods such as byte-pair encoding (BPE) balance vocabulary size against sequence length.” (Sequence length: how many tokens a text turns into.) BPE glues the most frequent pairs because chunks that appear all the time are worth having as one piece. Rare things get built from parts.

Analogy Think of LEGO. One special brick per object (a “castle brick”, a “car brick”) would mean millions of bricks and nothing new could be built. Only tiny 1×1 bricks would make every model take forever. LEGO sells a fixed box of common in-between pieces, and you can build almost anything from them. A tokenizer’s vocabulary is that box.

This gives four benefits, the ones the 2025 graders looked for:

  • Rare words: any new word, name or typo can be built from known pieces, so there are no “unknown words”.
  • Efficient computing: the fixed vocabulary keeps the model’s first step (turning each token into its list of numbers) and last step (picking the next token from the list) a manageable size.
  • Generalisation (using what was learned on some words for related ones): shared pieces link words like “grow”, “growing” and “growth”.
  • Many languages: one vocabulary can handle many languages and scripts. Handling many languages is called multilingual.

When to use it, and when not

  • Every LLM uses tokens; it is not a choice you make. What you use in a business case is the consequence: “prices, context limits, and speed are all measured in tokens.” Providers charge per token, and the context window (the most text a model can read at once) is counted in tokens. A long Norwegian contract costs more and fills the window faster than the same text in English.
  • Where tokens break: the model never sees single letters or digits, so it is weak at spelling, counting letters (“how many r’s in strawberry?”) and arithmetic. Some languages need more tokens for the same meaning, which means a higher bill and less room in the window.
  • Compared with “token” in bag-of-words: in bag-of-words (an older, simpler way to turn text into numbers: counting how often each word appears and ignoring word order), a token was a whole word cut out at the spaces. In LLMs, a token is a learned subword piece. Same word, two meanings, so say which one you mean.
  • Compared with stemming: stemming chops words down to a rough root by fixed rules (“easily” becomes “easili”). It throws information away, to shrink the word list for bag-of-words. BPE keeps all the text: glue the tokens back together and you get the exact original.

What the exam asks

2025 Q4(c) (5 points): “Describe in general terms what a token is, how it differs from words, and why we might want to use them”

Model answer

A token is the basic unit of text that a language model reads and predicts. Tokenization breaks raw text into these pieces, and each token is mapped to a vector (an embedding), so the token set defines which units of text become computable vectors. Tokens differ from words because they are usually sub-word pieces: a frequent word may be one token, while a rare or long word is split into several (e.g. “unbelievable” → un | believ | able, “strawberry” → st | raw | berry). Spaces, punctuation and parts of numbers can also be tokens. Tokenizers such as Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) are learned from data by starting with characters and repeatedly merging the most frequent adjacent pair until a target vocabulary size (e.g. ~100k–200k tokens) is reached.

We use tokens because they balance vocabulary size against sequence length. Rare, new or misspelled words can still be represented from known pieces, so there are no “unknown words”. The fixed vocabulary keeps the model’s input and output layers manageable and computation efficient. Shared sub-word pieces help the model generalise (e.g. across “grow”, “growing”, “growth”). And many languages and scripts can be handled with one vocabulary, which makes multilingual modelling easier. A practical consequence is that cost, speed and context-window limits are measured in tokens, and languages such as Norwegian need more tokens per word than English.

In plain words: a token is a chunk of text, often a piece of a word, that becomes a list of numbers. Common words stay whole and rare words get split into known pieces, so no word is ever unknown, the list stays a sensible size, related words are linked, and many languages fit in one list.

Explain how BPE works. (likely short question)

Model answer

Start from single characters, with an end-of-word marker. Count every pair of neighbouring symbols. Merge the most frequent pair into one new token and replace it everywhere. Repeat until the vocabulary reaches the target size. The vocabulary is learned from data, and it balances vocabulary size against sequence length.

In plain words: begin with letters, keep gluing together the two neighbours you see side by side most often, and stop when your list of pieces is big enough.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Defining a token as “a word”. That is the bag-of-words meaning, not the LLM meaning.
  • Giving only the “what” and forgetting the “why”. Q4(c) has three parts: what a token is, how it differs from a word, and why we use tokens.
  • Saying tokens are always smaller than words. Common words are one token.
  • Saying BPE uses a dictionary written by hand. It is learned from data.
  • Confusing tokenization with stemming. Stemming loses information; tokenization keeps all the text.

Key terms to use

  • token: a small piece of text
  • tokenizer: the tool that cuts text into tokens
  • subword: a piece smaller than a whole word
  • vocabulary / token IDs: the fixed token list and their numbers
  • byte-pair encoding (BPE): builds tokens by merging frequent pairs
  • most frequent pair: the two neighbours seen together most
  • vocabulary size vs sequence length: bigger list, fewer tokens per text
  • rare/unknown words: built from smaller known pieces
  • multilingual: one vocabulary works across languages
  • measured in tokens: prices and limits count tokens, not words

True/False traps

  • “The input to a Large Language Model is actually not words, but tokens.” (2025, T/F c) → True. Why: the tokenizer cuts text into pieces and the model reads those pieces; “actually” is there only to make you doubt yourself.
  • “one token always equals one word” → False. Why: as “Tokens are not words” above says, a common word can be one token, but “strawberry” is three pieces: st | raw | berry.
  • “BPE merges the least frequent pair” → False. Why: BPE glues the most frequent pair, because chunks that appear all the time are worth having as one piece; rare things get built from parts.
  • “In BPE, each step merges the most frequent pair of adjacent symbols.” → True. Why: “adjacent symbols” means two pieces sitting next to each other, and the most common such pair is glued at each step.
  • “the same sentence costs the same number of tokens in every language” → False. Why: token counts depend on language; the tokenizer’s pieces fit some languages better than others, so the same sentence can cost more in Norwegian than in English.
  • “A Norwegian sentence and its English translation use the same number of tokens.” → False. Why: as in the example above, a Norwegian sentence needs more tokens than its English translation, because Norwegian words get chopped into more pieces.
  • “Stemming and BPE tokenization both keep all the information in the original text.” → False. Why: stemming throws parts of words away; BPE only cuts, so the pieces glue back into the exact original.

16. Self-attention and the Transformer

On the exam: asked in 2025 as Q4(b) positional information (5 points), Q4(d) the motivation for self-attention (5 points), and True/False (a) and (i) (2 points each). That is 14 points.

The point of it

Words change meaning with their neighbours. Self-attention lets every word look around its sentence and update its own meaning from the words that matter. It is the heart of the Transformer, the design behind every modern LLM (a program like ChatGPT that writes text by guessing the next word, over and over).

How it works, in plain words

The problem. “We sat on the river bank” and “I put my salary in the bank”: same word, two meanings. You know which one from the words around it. Sometimes the clue is far away: “The animal didn’t cross the street because it was too tired.” To understand “it”, you must look back and decide: the animal or the street? (The animal. Streets don’t get tired.)

Words as lists of numbers. The model first cuts text into tokens (words or parts of words) and turns each token into an embedding: a list of numbers that stands for its meaning, like coordinates on a map. But this first lookup gives “bank” the same list in every sentence. What we want is a contextual embedding: a list for “bank” that changes with its neighbours. Say one sentence is about a bank next to a pond, and another says the bank “had issued a lot of credit”. The first “bank” should end up close to “water”, and the second close to “credit”.

Self-attention as a library search. Each word gets three new lists of numbers, made from its embedding by three tables the model learns during training (this is called projecting each token into a query, a key and a value):

  • the query: the word’s question, “what am I looking for?” Like the question you bring to a library.
  • the key: the word’s label, “what do I offer?” Like the label on a book’s spine.
  • the value: the word’s content, what gets passed on. Like the text inside the book.

Each word compares its query with every word’s key. The comparison is called a dot product: a quick way of measuring how well two lists of numbers match. A good match gives a high compatibility score. A rule called softmax turns the scores into shares that add up to 100%, the attention weights. Then the word mixes in the other words’ values in those shares, a weighted average, like a smoothie that is mostly banana with a bit of strawberry. The result is a new list for the word that carries its context. Back to the tired-animal sentence: “it” gives most of its attention to “animal” (made-up shares to picture it: animal 70%, street 10%, the rest 20%), so its new list carries a lot of “animal”. This picture is called the database or information-retrieval analogy: you match what you are looking for against the labels, then fetch the contents of the items that match. The 2025 graders looked for this analogy.

Two kinds of self-attention: causal and bidirectional.

  • Causal attention: each word may look only at itself and the words before it. The later words are hidden, which is called masking (“masking out the future”). Like reading with a sheet of paper covering everything to the right. In practice, the scores of the later words are set to minus infinity (written −∞, a value lower than any number) before softmax, so their share of attention comes out as exactly zero. Causal attention is needed when a model writes text one word at a time. While training to guess the next word, it must not peek at the answer: “if we know the future, it is easy to predict it.”
  • Bidirectional attention: each word looks both ways, at the whole sentence. It suits a model that only needs to understand a finished text.
  • GPT-style models (which write text) use causal attention; BERT-style models (which read and understand text) use bidirectional attention [Not in course files — general knowledge].

Multi-head attention: several readers. One full round of the library search is called a head. Multi-head attention runs several heads at the same time, each with its own query, key and value tables. So each head is free to notice a different kind of link: grammar (syntactic: who did what to whom) or meaning (semantic: what “it” refers to). Think of a team of editors reading the same text: one checks grammar, one checks meaning, one checks names. The heads’ results are glued together and brought back to the original length. It is richer, not slower: the heads run side by side (in parallel), and each works on a smaller slice of the numbers, so the total work is about the same as one big head [Not in course files — general knowledge]. So multi-head attention does not make estimation (another word for training a model) slower or less efficient.

The Transformer block. After attention comes a small extra calculation on each word, the feed-forward network. Its job, in the standard description: it “Adds non-linearity to the mix (self-attention is linear operations)”. In plain words: it lets the model capture curved, more complex patterns, not only straight-line ones. A few tidying steps keep training steady. Attention plus these steps is one Transformer block. Real models stack many blocks, and each one makes the word lists richer in meaning.

Positional encoding: seat numbers. Attention looks at all the words at the same time. Nothing in it says which word came first. The fix is a positional encoding (also called positional embedding): a list of numbers for each place in the sentence (“1st place”, “2nd place” and so on). It has the same length as a word’s list and is added to it slot by slot, not glued on the end (gluing on the end is called concatenating). Like stamping a seat number onto each ticket. The result is often written “word + position”. The original Transformer built these lists from smooth up-and-down waves, called sinusoidal (from sine and cosine).

Why it works: the reasoning

  • Meaning comes from context. So let each word borrow from the words that are relevant to it, weighted by how relevant they are.
  • Distance does not matter. Every word can look straight at every other allowed word. Links between far-apart words (long-range dependencies) are captured “without being limited by the sequence’s distance”.
  • Speed. All words are handled at once. Self-attention “allows for processing data in parallel, rather than sequentially. Implies significant improvements in training efficiency”.
  • You can see its work. The attention weights show which words a word paid attention to. This gives some interpretability.
  • Why positions must be added. Shuffle the words, and each word still finds the same partners with the same weights [Not in course files — general knowledge]. But order changes meaning: “‘The cat chased the dog’ and ‘The dog chased the cat’ have the same words but entirely different meanings.”

When to use it, and when not

  • Use causal attention when the job is writing: a chatbot, drafting emails, summarising reports.
  • Use bidirectional attention when the job is understanding a finished text: sorting reviews into positive and negative, or classifying news articles.
  • Don’t forget the costs: every word compares itself with every other word, so doubling the length of a text gives about four times as many comparisons [Not in course files — general knowledge]. Very long documents get expensive.
  • Without positional encoding, attention ignores word order completely.
  • Compared with word2vec: word2vec is an older method that learns one fixed list per word, whatever the sentence. Self-attention gives a context-specific list, so “bank” by a river and “bank” with a salary end up different.
  • Compared with reading one word after another: older designs worked through a sentence in order [Not in course files — general knowledge]. Self-attention handles all words in parallel, which trains faster and links distant words directly.

What the exam asks

2025 Q4(d) (5 points): “Describe in intuitive terms the motivation for the self-attention layer within the Transformer block”

Model answer

The meaning of a word depends on its context, e.g. “bank” next to “water” vs. next to “credit”, or “it” referring back to “the animal”. Self-attention lets every token build a new, contextual representation by looking at the other tokens in the sequence and weighting them by relevance. It works like information retrieval from a database. Each token is projected into a query (what this token is looking for), a key (what each token offers to be matched against) and a value (the content that is retrieved). The dot product of a token’s query with every key gives a compatibility score. A softmax turns the scores into attention weights that are positive and sum to one. The new embedding is the weighted sum of the values. So a token attends strongly to the few earlier (causal) or earlier-and-later (bidirectional) tokens that matter for its meaning, with varying weights. The motivation is that this captures syntactic and semantic dependencies regardless of how far apart words are (long-range dependencies), it can be computed for all tokens in parallel rather than word by word (faster training), and the attention weights give some interpretability. Several such heads in parallel (multi-head attention) can capture different kinds of relationships.

In plain words: a word’s meaning depends on its neighbours, so each word runs a little search. Its question (query) is checked against every word’s label (key); the “dot product” is the match score, and “softmax” turns the scores into shares that add up to 100%. The word then mixes in the content (values) of the best matches. The graders wanted three things: query, key and value named; the database-search analogy; and each word attending to others with different weights.

2025 Q4(b) (5 points): “Why do we typically add positional information to the embeddings entering a Large Language Model?”

Model answer

The self-attention mechanism processes all tokens in the sequence simultaneously (in parallel) and computes attention from dot products between token vectors. Nothing in this calculation tells the model in which order the tokens came. Word order is crucial for meaning: “The cat chased the dog” and “The dog chased the cat” contain the same words but mean different things. Therefore a positional embedding, a vector that encodes the position of each token, is added to each token embedding before the first Transformer block, so the input becomes “word + position” and the model can use order information. In the original Transformer the positional vectors are sinusoidal functions of the position, \(PE_{(pos,2i)}=\sin\big(pos/10000^{2i/d_{model}}\big)\) and \(PE_{(pos,2i+1)}=\cos\big(pos/10000^{2i/d_{model}}\big)\). These give each position a unique code, allow relative distances between words to be represented, and extend to sequences longer than those seen in training.

What the formula means: each position gets its own pattern of smooth waves, some fast and some slow, so no two positions look alike.

In plain words: attention compares all words at once, so on its own it cannot tell which came first. Order changes meaning (who chased whom), so a “seat number” is added to each word’s list before it goes in. The graders wanted two points: attention does not see the order, and order is crucial for meaning.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Explaining attention without naming query, key and value. The 2025 graders asked for them.
  • Saying causal masking hides past words. It hides future words.
  • Saying multi-head attention is slower, or that self-attention reads one word at a time. It works on all words in parallel.
  • Saying positional encoding is needed “because words have different meanings”. That is attention’s job. Positional encoding is about order.
  • Saying the position list is glued on the end. It is added.
  • Saying word2vec-style vectors already depend on context. Each word gets one fixed list.

Key terms to use

  • contextual embedding: word numbers that change with the sentence
  • self-attention: each word looks at the other words
  • query, key, value: what I seek, my label, what I share
  • information-retrieval analogy: attention works like a database search
  • attention weights: shares of attention adding to 100%
  • causal vs bidirectional: look back only vs look both ways
  • masking the future: hiding later words during training
  • multi-head attention: several attention readers side by side
  • long-range dependencies: links between far-apart words
  • positional encoding, added: seat numbers added to word numbers
  • sinusoidal: built from sine and cosine waves

True/False traps

  • “In the Transformer’s self-attention mechanism, you can have two types of attention; causal- and bidirectional-attention.” (2025, T/F a) → True. Why: these are the two kinds described above: causal attention looks back only (for writing text), and bidirectional attention looks both ways (for understanding a finished text).
  • “Multi-head attention mechanisms makes estimation of Large Language Models slower and less efficient” (2025, T/F i) → False. Why: “estimation” means training. The heads run side by side, in parallel, each on a smaller slice, and each can spot a different kind of link, so multi-head attention is an improvement, not a slowdown.
  • “Masking hides earlier words” → False. Why: masking hides the future (later) words, so the model can’t peek at the word it is learning to guess; earlier words stay visible.
  • “In causal attention, the scores for future words are set to −∞ before the softmax.” → True. Why: this is how masking is done: minus infinity is lower than any number, so when softmax turns the scores into shares, future words get a share of exactly zero.
  • “The feed-forward layer adds non-linearity” → True. Why: the feed-forward network “Adds non-linearity”: it lets the model capture curved, more complex patterns, not only the straight-line ones attention gives.
  • “Positional vectors are concatenated” → False. Why: “concatenated” means glued on the end; the position numbers are added slot by slot instead, like stamping a seat number onto each ticket.
  • “Positional embeddings are added to the word embeddings.” → True. Why: each word’s numbers get its position’s numbers added, giving “word + position”.

17. How an LLM picks its next word: input, output and temperature

On the exam: not a full question in 2025, but True/False (c) in 2025 was about tokens. This topic is a rich source of likely True/False items (temperature, greedy picking, BERT’s 512 tokens, cross-attention) and short “explain” questions. It also helps the reflection: which setting to use for sorting texts and which for creative writing.

The point of it

A large language model (LLM), such as ChatGPT, is a giant autocomplete. It writes one small piece of text at a time, and each time it guesses what should come next. This section covers what goes in, what comes out, and how one next word gets picked from all the possible ones.

How it works, in plain words

Step 1: text in, cut into tokens. The model does not read words. It reads tokens: small pieces of text. A token is often a whole word (“cat”), sometimes part of a word (“unhappy” might be “un” + “happy”), or a punctuation mark. Pieces let the model handle rare or new words, because it can build them from pieces it already knows, and they keep its list of pieces to a manageable size. That fixed list of all the tokens a model knows is its vocabulary, like the word list of a dictionary.

Step 2: each token gets its meaning-numbers. A computer can only work with numbers. So each token is looked up in a big table and swapped for its own list of numbers, its embedding: a kind of number code for what the token means. Think of a phone book: name in, number out. The table is called the embedding matrix (a matrix is a table of numbers, like a spreadsheet). The lookup works with a one-hot row: a row of zeros with a single 1 that marks the token. When this row is multiplied by the table, every 0 wipes out its row of the table and the single 1 keeps one. So the multiplication picks out exactly that token’s row, like holding one key in a wall of lockers: only one locker opens. Nobody writes the numbers in the table by hand. They are set during training, the long learning phase where the model’s adjustable numbers (its weights) are tuned.

Step 3: add “where am I?” numbers. “Dog bites man” is not “man bites dog”. But the lookup gives “dog” the same numbers wherever it stands. So each position in the text (1st, 2nd, 3rd…) gets its own list of numbers too, a positional embedding, like a seat number at a cinema. It is added to the token’s numbers, number by number, not glued on the end. Gluing two lists end to end is called concatenating, and the exam may use that word as a trick: word and position numbers are added, not concatenated. Now each token carries both “which word am I?” and “where am I?”.

Step 4: the layers. These lists then pass through many Transformer layers: processing stages stacked one after another, like rounds of a group discussion. In each round, every token looks at the other tokens and updates its own numbers. This looking-around is called attention. When the model reads “it”, for example, it glances back to see what “it” refers to.

Step 5: a chance for every possible next token. At the end, the model takes its final notes on the last token, which by now sum up the whole text so far. It compares those notes with every token in its vocabulary and gives each one a score: how well would this token fit next? The same lookup table from step 2 is reused for this scoring. Using one table in both directions is called weight tying, like one English–French dictionary used both ways. A final step called softmax then turns the scores into chances that add up to 100%. The output is this full set of chances, a probability distribution, like a weather forecast: 70% sun, 20% cloud, 10% rain.

A size limit on the input. The most tokens a model can read at once is its context window (or maximum length), like the size of a desk. It depends on the model. The original BERT, a well-known model family built to understand text, reads at most 512 tokens. The GPT family, the one ChatGPT belongs to, reads “some thousand tokens”, and newer models from a few thousand up to about one million. The limit exists mainly because of computer memory: attention compares every token with every other token. If your text is too long, the part that doesn’t fit may be cut off without warning (truncated), which can change the answer, or you get an error.

Step 6: pick one token, add it, go again. The rule for picking is called decoding. These are the main rules:

  • Greedy decoding: always pick the top token. Like always ordering the most popular dish on the menu. The same input gives the same output every time; a rule like that is called deterministic. Downside: the text is “generic and often quite repetitive”.
  • Random sampling: draw a token at random, weighted by its chance. Like drawing a ball from a bag where most balls are red, some blue, a few green. Downside: low-chance words get picked too often.
  • Top-k sampling: keep only the k most likely tokens (k is a number you choose), throw the rest away, rescale the kept chances so they add up to 100% again (re-normalising), then draw among them. Downside: k stays fixed, but sometimes only 2 options make sense and sometimes 50 do.
  • Temperature sampling: reshape the chances before drawing (see below).
  • Top-p sampling is named in the course material but not explained there. [Not in course files — general knowledge] It keeps the smallest group of top tokens whose chances add up to a share you choose, such as 90%, so the number kept changes with the situation.

The chosen token is stuck onto the end of the text, and the whole machine runs again for the next one: “So long and thanks for” → “all” → “the” … until the answer is done.

The point of temperature: it’s a creativity dial. When an AI like ChatGPT writes, it picks one word at a time, and for each next word it has given every possible word a chance. After “I walked my…” the chances might be dog 70%, cat 20%, banana 10% (made-up numbers). Temperature changes those chances just before the word is picked. Low temperature: the favourite gets even more likely, so “dog” almost every time. Safe and predictable, but repetitive. Good for a customer-service bot. High temperature: the chances get flatter, so “cat” and even “banana” come up more. Varied and creative, but more mistakes. Good for brainstorming. It doesn’t change what the model has learned; it only changes how the next word is picked. Exam: “A higher temperature makes the output more random” → True.

The dial has a middle point. At temperature 1 the chances are left exactly as the model gave them. Below 1 they get sharper: the favourite wins even more often. Above 1 they get flatter: the underdogs catch up. At temperature 0 the model always takes the top token, which is greedy decoding, so the same input always gives the same output.

Encoder and decoder: a second meaning of “decoding”. These words also name two parts of a model.

  • The encoder reads and understands. It reads the whole input and gives every input word numbers that reflect its meaning in this sentence.
  • The decoder writes. It produces the output one token at a time, using what it has written so far.

A model with both is an encoder–decoder model; the original Transformer was built this way for translation (“I am a dog” → “Jeg er en hund”). Inside a decoder, each token may look only at itself and earlier tokens, never later ones, like reading a book with the next pages glued shut. This is causal attention.

Cross-attention is how the two parts talk. It is the step where the writing part (decoder) looks back at the reading part’s (encoder’s) notes, like a translator glancing back at the original sentence while writing the translation. Attention always uses three things. The query is the question a token asks: “what am I looking for?” The keys are labels on the notes, used to find the matching ones. The values are the contents of those notes, which get blended into the answer. In cross-attention, the queries come from the decoder (the writer brings the question) and the keys and values come from the encoder (the reader’s notes supply the labels and the content). Swapping these two sides is a favourite exam trap.

BERT-type models are encoders: each word sees the words on both sides (bidirectional), and they are used to understand text, for example to judge whether a review is positive. GPT-type models are decoders: they see only earlier words and are used to write. [Not in course files — general knowledge] The course material lists these features without pairing them with BERT and GPT; the pairing is standard knowledge.

Why it works: the reasoning

  • Numbers carry meaning. A computer can only calculate with numbers, so each token needs a number code. Because training sets these codes, tokens used in similar ways end up with similar codes.
  • Order matters, so position is added. Without the seat numbers, “dog bites man” and “man bites dog” would look the same to the model.
  • The last token speaks for the text. After all the layers, the last token’s notes have taken in everything before it, so they are the right place to guess what comes next.
  • A chance for every token, not one answer. Because the model gives every token a chance, you can choose how bold the pick should be. That is why the same trained model can write a safe invoice summary or a playful slogan: only the picking rule changes.

When to use it, and when not

  • Use temperature 0 (greedy) when there is one right answer and another program reads the result: pulling the amount off an invoice, sorting reviews into labels, filling in a form. Same input, same output.
  • Use a higher temperature when you want variety: ten campaign slogans, brainstorming product names.
  • Don’t use a high temperature for facts. Flatter chances let odd words through, and at very high values the text turns to nonsense.
  • Don’t use greedy for creative work. It gives generic, repetitive text.
  • Watch the context window. A long contract may be cut off without warning, so check the model’s limit before feeding in long documents.
  • Compared with random sampling: greedy is safe but dull; random sampling is varied but lets too many unlikely words in. Top-k and temperature sit in between.
  • Compared with a BERT-type model: pick a GPT-type (decoder) model to write text; pick a BERT-type (encoder) model to understand and sort text.

What the exam asks

Why might a company set temperature to 0 when extracting invoice amounts, but not when writing campaign slogans?

Model answer

The model’s next-token probabilities are reshaped by \(\mathrm{softmax}(u/\tau)\). A low τ sharpens them, and at 0 the model always takes the top token (greedy), so the same input gives the same output. That predictability is what you want when one correct answer exists and another program reads the result. For slogans you want variety, so a higher τ flattens the distribution and lets less obvious words through. The cost is a higher risk of nonsense.

The formula means: the model’s raw scores for each possible next token (\(u\)) are divided by the temperature (\(\tau\)) and then turned into chances that add up to 100%.

In plain words: temperature 0 means the model always picks its top word, so the same invoice always gives the same amount, which is good when there is one right answer and a computer reads it. For slogans you turn the temperature up for fresh ideas, at the price of the odd piece of nonsense.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Saying “high temperature = more focused”. It is the opposite: high temperature means more random.
  • Saying the input is words. It is tokens (words or word pieces, like “play” + “ing”).
  • Forgetting that top-k re-normalises before drawing: after keeping the top k, their chances are rescaled to add up to 100% again.
  • Claiming the model uses every token’s final notes to guess the next word. It uses only the last token’s.
  • Mixing up which side supplies the queries in cross-attention. It is the decoder.
  • Presenting top-p as defined in the course material. It is not explained there.

Key terms to use

  • token: a word or a piece of a word
  • embedding: a token’s list of meaning-numbers
  • one-hot row: zeros with one 1, picks a row
  • positional embedding, added: place-in-text numbers added on top
  • context window: most tokens the model reads at once
  • truncation: cutting off text beyond the limit
  • softmax over the vocabulary: scores turned into chances adding to 100%
  • weight tying: same table used going in and out
  • greedy decoding: always pick the most likely token
  • random sampling: draw the next token by its chances
  • top-k: draw only from the k likeliest tokens
  • temperature: dial, low = predictable, high = varied
  • deterministic: same input, same output every time
  • encoder: the part that reads and understands
  • decoder: the part that writes, token by token
  • causal attention: each token sees only earlier tokens
  • cross-attention: decoder looks back at the encoder’s notes

True/False traps

  • “The input to a Large Language Model is actually not words, but tokens.” (2025, item c) → True. Why: as step 1 explains, the model reads tokens, which can be whole words, pieces of words or punctuation marks.
  • “A higher temperature makes the output more random.” → True. Why: turning the creativity dial up flattens the chances, so less likely words get picked more often.
  • “Higher temperature makes the output more deterministic.” → False. Why: “deterministic” means the same answer every time, and a higher temperature does the opposite: it makes the output more random.
  • “Setting the temperature above 1 makes the next-word chances sharper.” → False. Why: temperature 1 leaves the chances as they are; above 1 flattens them so the underdogs catch up, and only below 1 sharpens them.
  • “At temperature 0 the model gives the same answer every time.” → True. Why: temperature 0 is greedy decoding, where the model always takes its top token, so there is no dice roll.
  • “Greedy decoding always picks the most likely word.” → True. Why: that is the definition of greedy decoding, like always ordering the most popular dish.
  • “The original BERT can take inputs of 512 tokens.” → True. Why: as the context-window paragraph says, 512 tokens is the most the original BERT can read at once.
  • “Word and position embeddings are concatenated.” → False. Why: as step 3 explains, the position numbers are added to the word’s numbers, not glued on the end (concatenated).
  • “Multiplying a one-hot row by the embedding matrix selects one row of that matrix.” → True. Why: as step 2 explains, every 0 wipes out its row and the single 1 keeps one, so the multiplication works as a lookup.
  • “Weight tying means the same table is used to build the input and to produce the output scores.” → True. Why: as step 5 explains, weight tying means one table is used in both directions, like one dictionary used both ways.
  • “In cross-attention, queries come from the encoder.” → False. Why: the queries (the questions) come from the decoder, the writing part; only the keys and values come from the encoder.
  • “In the decoder’s cross-attention layer, the keys and values come from the encoder.” → True. Why: the writer brings the questions, and the reader’s (encoder’s) notes supply the labels (keys) and the contents (values).

18. Fine-tuning vs prompting (zero-shot and few-shot)

On the exam: asked in 2025, Q4(e) (5 points: “the difference between fine-tuning approaches and prompt engineering”) and True/False (j) (2 points, on zero-shot). It is also the main toolbox for the business-case reflection.

The point of it

You have a ready-made model that “knows language” but not your job. You can either send it back to school on your own examples (fine-tuning), or write it a better set of instructions (prompting). One line sums it up: “Uploading a document changes the context. Fine-tuning changes the weights.”

How it works, in plain words

What the model is made of. A large language model (LLM), such as ChatGPT, is a giant machine full of adjustable dials. Each dial is one number, called a weight or parameter (the two words mean the same here). The dials were set in a long first round of training on huge amounts of ordinary text, called pre-training. A pre-trained model knows language, but it has never seen your task.

What you send it. The text you give the model is the prompt: instructions, examples, documents and your question. Everything the model can see in one request is its context. The context disappears after the answer; the next request starts fresh. The weights, on the other hand, stay fixed while you chat: nothing you type in a conversation changes them. Only training changes them.

So there are two different things you can change: the context (what the model reads this time) or the weights (the dials inside it).

Take a bank that wants each customer complaint sorted into “fees”, “app” or “loans”.

Prompting: write a better brief. You change only what you say. The skill of writing prompts that get good, reliable answers is prompt engineering. Common moves:

  • Zero-shot [Not in course files — general knowledge]: instructions only, no solved examples. “Sort this complaint into one of: fees, app, loans. Reply with one word.” The “zero” means zero examples.
  • Few-shot: add one or a few solved examples to the prompt, and the model copies the pattern. “Example: ‘The app crashes when I log in.’ → app.” The examples can sit in the system prompt (below) or next to the question; either way it is few-shot, and either way they are only something the model reads.
  • A system prompt: standing instructions sent before the user’s text, like house rules on a staff noticeboard, often with an exact output format and the right tone (style of voice).
  • Chain-of-thought [Not in course files — general knowledge]: asking the model to reason step by step before its final answer.
  • Adding relevant documents to the prompt (retrieval).

In none of these does a single dial move. Chat models already had extra training by their maker to follow instructions (they are instruction-tuned), which is why a good brief works.

Fine-tuning: send it back to school. Here you change the model itself.

  1. Collect complaints that staff have already sorted by hand. Examples with the right answer attached are labelled data: training data that is already classified.
  2. Bolt a small new output part onto the top of the model whose only job is to choose between your bins. This is a problem-specific head.
  3. Train it: show a complaint, let the model guess, measure how wrong it was, nudge the dials, repeat. Learning from labelled examples like this is supervised training.
  4. Choose which dials may move: only the new head’s dials, with all the old ones locked (frozen), or every dial in the model.

An example, step by step. Say you run a film website and want to sort thousands of reviews into happy (positive) and unhappy (negative) ones. The input is one review, such as “entirely predictable and lacks energy”. The output you want is two chances that add up to 100%: how likely the review is positive, and how likely it is negative. For this review, “negative” should win.

  • You start from a ready-made BERT-type model: a model family built to read and understand text rather than to write it. A model’s reading part is called an encoder, so BERT is an encoder.
  • Before the review goes in, a special marker called [CLS] is put at the very start: “[CLS] entirely predictable and lacks energy”. It is not a real word. It is a placeholder whose job is to hold a summary. After the model has read the whole review, the list of numbers it holds at the marker’s position (its embedding) acts as a summary of the whole text.
  • On top you bolt a head: a small extra final layer that turns the model’s output into the answer you need. Here it is a softmax layer, which turns that summary into two chances, positive and negative, that add up to 100%.
  • You then train on reviews that people have already tagged positive or negative. Retraining the head (and maybe the rest of the model) on such examples is what the exam calls re-estimating the parameters: the dials get new values.

This is the example the lectures used, and the 2025 model answer below mentions the [CLS] marker, so it is worth knowing.

Fine-tuning is one case of transfer learning: reusing what a model learned on one job to help with another, like a violin player learning the viola much faster than a beginner.

A third route: borrow the model’s notes. You can also ask the model only for its number summaries of your texts (embeddings) and train your own small sorting program on them. The big model’s dials stay untouched.

Why it works: the reasoning

  • Fine-tuning works because the hard part is already done. Pre-training taught the model language. Your labelled examples only need to point that knowledge at one job, so far fewer examples are needed than building a model from scratch. Training only the new head is cheap because it is a tiny fraction of the model’s dials.
  • Prompting works because the model’s next-word guesses depend on everything in its context. Clear instructions, examples and documents change what the most likely answer is, without touching what the model has learned.
  • The trade-off is control against effort. Changing the weights gives a specialist that behaves the same way every time, but costs data, computer power (often called compute) and skill. Changing the context is fast and cheap, but the result depends on how well the prompt is written.

When to use it, and when not

  • Use prompting when you need a quick start, the task keeps changing, you have few or no labelled examples, and a capable general model is available. Keep a small hand-sorted set to check quality.
  • Don’t rely on prompting when you need the same behaviour every time at huge volume. It can be inconsistent, you pay for every token (text piece) on every request, a small model run on your own laptop (a local model) can take 5–30 seconds per text, and only so much fits in the context window (the most text the model can read at once).
  • Use fine-tuning when the task is stable, the volume is high, and you have (or can create) labelled data. You get a consistent, cheaper-to-run specialist.
  • Don’t use fine-tuning when you lack labelled data, computer power or skills, or when the categories keep changing, since every change means training again. Full fine-tuning (moving every dial) also risks the model memorising its examples instead of learning the pattern (over-fitting).
  • Neither guarantees correct answers. Check results against a sample people have sorted by hand and against a simple benchmark (a basic method to compare against, like “always guess the biggest bin”).
  • Compared with embeddings + your own model: the LLM stays unchanged, the small model is cheap to run and easy to check, but you still need labels for it.
  • Compared with RAG (a search step that pastes relevant documents into the prompt): RAG is a kind of prompting. It changes what the model reads, not the model, and suits answers that must rest on your own up-to-date documents.

What the exam asks

2025, Q4(e) (5 points): “In the context of Large Language Models, describe shortly the difference between fine-tuning approaches and prompt engineering.”

Model answer

Fine-tuning is a form of transfer learning: we take a pre-trained LLM, typically add a problem-specific head (e.g. a softmax layer that classifies sentiment from the [CLS] embedding of a BERT-type encoder), and re-estimate either all the model’s parameters or only the parameters of the new head on labelled data for the new task. It therefore changes the weights. It requires supervised training data, compute and technical work, but can give a cheaper, specialised and consistent model for a narrow, high-volume task.

Prompt engineering uses the pre-trained (instruction-tuned) model as is. No weights are re-estimated; we only improve the input, the context, so the model better understands the task. Examples: a clear instruction and role/system prompt, appropriate context and tone, an explicit output format, a few worked examples (few-shot), asking for step-by-step reasoning (chain-of-thought), or adding relevant documents (retrieval). It is fast and needs little or no labelled data, but results depend on the prompt and must be validated.

In short: fine-tuning changes what the model is (its parameters); prompting changes what the model sees (its context).

In plain words: fine-tuning takes a ready-made model, maybe bolts a small extra piece on top, and trains it more on examples with the right answers, so its inner numbers change. Prompt engineering leaves the model alone and writes a better request; it is quick and needs almost no data, but you must check the results.

19. Using LLMs in practice, including RAG

On the exam: not asked directly in 2025, but it is ready-made material for the business-case reflection (2025 Block 5, 20 points) and for True/False items on RAG, temperature and token cost.

The point of it

An LLM writes fluent text, but it does not look things up. To use it safely in a business, you pick a job where mistakes can be caught, hand it the right documents, and check its work. RAG is the “hand it the right documents” part: giving the model your own files to look things up in before it answers.

How it works, in plain words

Where firms use LLMs. A large language model (LLM), such as ChatGPT, is a program that writes text by guessing a likely next piece over and over. Five common uses are:

  • Customer support: answer questions from company knowledge and handle routine requests.
  • Software development: write features, find bugs, run tests.
  • Document and data analysis: pull information out of reports and tables, compare documents.
  • Research and reporting: search sources, sum up the evidence, draft reports with references.
  • Business workflows: prepare documents, update records, coordinate tasks across systems.

How much is left to the machine “depends on task reliability, permissions, and human review”.

Four ways to run one. A person can use a chat app and check each answer. Your own software can send text to the provider over the internet and get answers back (API integration). An agent lets the model plan, use tools and repeat steps by itself. Or you run a free model on your own computer (local deployment), possible because some makers publish the model’s learned numbers (open weights). The key point: “The mode determines the cost structure, where data goes, and who is accountable for errors.”

What makes a good use case. Five things matter: a clear task (what goes in, what should come out, what counts as success), available context (the documents and data needed), verifiable results (answers that are easy to check against sources or rules), manageable errors (start where mistakes can be caught before they cause harm) and business value (count the cost of setting it up, checking it and keeping it running).

Picture two questions for every task: how bad is a mistake, and how hard is it to check? Drafting marketing copy: low harm, easy to check, so check before publishing. Pulling invoice amounts for payment: high harm but easy to check, so compare with the original records. Approving unusual payments: high harm and hard to check, so a person must approve. Summing up customer feedback: low harm but hard to check, so read some examples yourself.

RAG, step by step. RAG stands for retrieval-augmented generation. “Retrieval” means finding text; “generation” means the model writing its answer. Say staff ask a chatbot about the company handbook.

  1. The handbook is cut into short slices, called chunks.
  2. A search tool, the retriever, finds the few chunks that best match the question.
  3. Those chunks are pasted into the prompt (the text the model is given), with an instruction like “Answer using ONLY the provided excerpts”. Telling the model to answer only from a text you supply is grounding, like an open-book exam where only the book on the desk is allowed.
  4. The model writes its answer, ideally at temperature 0. Temperature is a creativity dial for how the model picks each next word: low is safe and predictable, high is varied but makes more mistakes. At 0 the model always takes its most likely next word, so the same question gives the same answer every time.

Why not paste in the whole handbook? The model can only read so much text at once; that limit is its context window, like the size of a desk. A big collection of documents does not fit, so a search step has to choose what goes on the desk.

Two kinds of retriever. A word-matching search, such as tf-idf (it scores a word highly when it is frequent in this chunk but rare across all chunks), only finds chunks that share the exact words of the question. A search built on dense embeddings (number lists that capture what a text means) can match meaning, so it can link “pensions” with “Social Security”. The small practice RAG system built for this course uses the first kind, tf-idf word matching, and names dense embeddings as the better choice; that is an easy True/False item.

Making the output usable. When answers feed into other software, they need an exact shape, such as JSON (a strict text layout like {"sentiment": "positive"}). Four steps make the output reliable: set temperature 0; write a system prompt (standing instructions before the user’s text) spelling out the exact output format; add one solved example (few-shot); and say what to do with edge cases, like a lukewarm review (“The app is fine, I guess”). Which labels are allowed is “a business decision, not a coding one”.

Why it works: the reasoning

The model predicts plausible text; it does not look things up. Without the facts in front of it, the most likely-sounding answer may be invented. Putting the right passage into its context makes the true answer the most likely one to write. That is why RAG helps, and also why it fails when the search hands over the wrong passage: the model will happily repeat whatever it was given. The model’s weights (the huge set of adjustable numbers it learned in training) stay exactly as they were: RAG only changes what the model reads this time. In one line: “Retrieval changes context; fine-tuning changes weights. Neither guarantees accuracy.”

When to use it, and when not

  • Use RAG when answers must rest on your own or up-to-date documents: a handbook chatbot, customer questions about policies, reports with source references.
  • Don’t use RAG when no lookup is needed, such as labelling the feeling of a review. A clear prompt or a trained classifier fits better.
  • Use temperature 0 when extracting facts or labelling texts; a higher value only when you want variety.
  • Compared with fine-tuning: RAG changes what the model reads each time; fine-tuning changes the model itself. RAG suits facts that change and must be quoted; fine-tuning suits one stable, high-volume behaviour.
  • Compared with a tf-idf retriever: an embedding retriever handles paraphrases (the same thing said in other words), while tf-idf matches literal words only.

Don’t use an LLM without planning for its main risks. These are the ones to name in a business case:

  • Hallucination: the model states something false with confidence, such as an invented quote. With no source it dodges or makes things up; the right source fixes it; the wrong source can be worse than none.
  • Retriever misses rewording: a word-matching search finds nothing when the question uses other words than the document. Test how often it finds the right chunk (its hit rate) on reworded questions, and consider embedding-based search.
  • Format failures: a few good test replies do not prove thousands will be. Four valid JSON replies out of four tries say little about 100,000 reviews. Build checks around the model: a program that tests each reply’s format, a validation sample (texts people have labelled by hand) and a benchmark to beat, such as Naive Bayes (a simple word-counting sorter) or always guessing the most common label.
  • Token cost: models read and charge per token (a word or word piece). The tool that cuts text into tokens was built mostly from English text, so Norwegian text, numbers and codes get cut into more pieces than English, so they cost more and fill the context window faster.
  • Privacy: sending customer texts to an outside provider moves personal data; running a model locally keeps it on your own machine, but a small local model is weaker and slower.

Second, the retriever can miss questions asked in other words. A tf-idf retriever only matches literal words, so “pension” will not find “retirement benefits”. In plain words: when the model doesn’t know the facts, it makes them up, so find the right handbook pages, paste them in and tell it to use only those. A word-matching search misses questions asked in different words, so use a search that understands meaning and test it.

20. Critical issues with LLMs (ESG)

On the exam: asked in 2025, Q4(a) (5 points): “Discuss shortly two” ESG issues of LLMs. The same issues make good pitfalls in the business-case reflection and easy True/False items.

The point of it

LLMs are useful, but they raise worries for the planet, for people, and about who holds power and which rules apply. The exam wants two of these worries, each explained: what it is, why it happens, and what follows from it.

How it works, in plain words

Two ways an LLM uses computers. A large language model (LLM), such as ChatGPT, writes text by guessing a likely next word, over and over. Building it is called training: a long, one-off job where thousands of computer chips tune the model’s numbers on huge amounts of text, for weeks. Using the finished model to answer is called inference, and it happens again for every question from every user. Many exam traps hide in the idea that only training costs anything.

Three headings: E, S and G. Firms sort risks that are not about money into three groups, ESG. Think of a new factory in town:

  • E (Environmental): it uses lots of electricity and water. Nature and resources.
  • S (Social): its products are mostly great but sometimes faulty, and a faulty product can hurt people. People and society.
  • G (Governance): one or two owners control it, it holds customers’ personal data, and the law sets rules for it. Rules, control and who is in charge.

The course does not use the word “ESG”. It asks four questions instead: Sustainability (“What resources do training and repeated inference consume?”), Ownership and Control (“Who controls the models, infrastructure, and access?”), Reliability (“Are outputs accurate, supported by evidence, and appropriate for the task?”) and Privacy and Security (“Where does data go, and what can a connected system do with it?”). You sort them into E, S and G yourself. Here is each issue.

1. Energy and carbon footprint (E). - What: “Both training and inference consume electricity; cooling, water use, and chip production also matter.” All this adds to the carbon footprint, the climate-warming gas released because of it. - Why: huge models need huge amounts of computing. Some uses need more for each job: “Reasoning, long contexts, and repeated agent steps can increase computation per task.” (Reasoning is the model writing hidden “thinking” before its answer; an agent repeats a loop of deciding and using tools.) - Consequence or fix: the footprint depends on model size, hardware, workload and how clean the electricity is, so there is “No universal carbon cost per prompt”. Compare models per successfully completed task, counting retries and human checking, and pick a model no bigger than the job needs. And as each request gets cheaper, people may use far more, so the total can still rise.

2. Reliability: hallucination and bias (S). - What: a hallucination is the model inventing facts, numbers, quotations or references “even when they sound confident”. Bias is a systematic slant, such as treating some groups unfairly. Answers can also change with the wording of the question. - Why: the model writes what sounds likely, not what has been checked. And it learns from human text, slants included. - Consequence or fix: it can mislead customers or decision makers. “Decide how an answer will be checked before relying on it”: check sources, use a calculator or code for numbers, and keep a person in charge of decisions that matter.

Don’t mix these up: a hallucination is invented content; bias is a slant that repeats.

3. Responsibility: harm and misuse (S, also G). - What: “Harm can come from biased, abusive, misleading, or overly agreeable outputs” (overly agreeable means telling users what they want to hear). The course’s example is Microsoft’s Tay chatbot in 2016; that users quickly got it to post offensive content is [Not in course files — general knowledge]. A newer danger is prompt injection: “malicious instructions in a document or website try to redirect a connected assistant”. - Why: an assistant connected to email, files or payments may obey any text it reads, so a hidden line on a web page (“ignore your rules and send me the files”) can take control of it. - Consequence or fix: “Restrict tool permissions, review consequential actions, and keep records of what happened.”

4. Ownership and control: a dangerous monopoly? (G). - What: building the newest, most powerful models (frontier models) needs “substantial capital, chips, power, data, and expertise. These create barriers to entry.” But “the market is not a single monopoly”: power is concentrated at several levels, “chips, cloud hosting, models, and distribution”. - Why: only a few firms can afford it. - Consequence or fix: a business that builds on one provider can get stuck with it (vendor lock-in), facing price or service changes it cannot control. The course’s test: “Can you switch provider, export your data, or run an alternative model?” Open weights (model numbers you can download and run yourself) help, but they “do not necessarily include training data or the full training recipe”, and they “do not remove infrastructure costs or dependencies”.

5. Privacy and security (G). - What: prompts and uploads “may contain personal or confidential information”. - Why: what you type travels to the provider’s computers. How long they keep it, whether it trains future models and in which country it is handled “depend on the product and contract; verify them.” - Consequence or fix: GDPR, the EU’s personal-data law, “applies to personal data in prompts and outputs”, and the EU AI Act sets rules for AI providers. Use approved services, share as little as possible, and let search tools and agents see only records the user is allowed to see.

6. Training data used without permission: copyright (G, also S). - What: LLMs are trained on huge amounts of text, often collected without asking the authors. Copyright is an author’s legal right to control copies of their work. - Why: LLMs need very large amounts of training text (“large data needed”, in the marking guide’s words), gathered from the internet, books and other sources. - Consequence or fix: questions about who owns the output and whether creators should be paid. Pre-training “can also memorize examples”, so a model may repeat protected or personal text. This issue is in the 2025 marking guide but not on the 2026 slides, so say where it comes from.

Why it works: the reasoning

Each worry comes straight from how LLMs are made. They need huge computing, so they use energy and water (E). They predict likely words rather than checked facts, and they learn from human text, so they can invent things and repeat slants (S). They cost so much to build that only a few firms can make them, and they read whatever you type, so power and data pile up with those firms (G). Linking each issue to its cause is what turns a list into a “discussion”.

When to use it, and when not

  • Use these issues when the exam asks about ESG or critical issues, and as pitfalls in the reflection: an LLM for customer texts brings hallucination, and sending those texts to an outside provider brings privacy and GDPR.
  • Use energy as your first issue. The 2025 marking guide names it. For the second, copyright (also in the guide) or any well-grounded one: reliability, ownership and control, privacy.
  • Don’t list six issues in a line each. “Discuss” means explain; two issues done well beat six named.
  • Don’t say only training costs energy. Every use costs energy too.
  • Compared across headings: privacy and prompt injection can go under S (harm to people) or G (rules and control). Either works if you say why.

Model answer

1. Environmental — energy use and carbon footprint. LLMs need very large amounts of electricity, both to train (huge models on huge datasets for weeks on specialised chips) and to run every time someone uses them (inference). Model answer

Ownership and control is a governance issue: training frontier models needs huge capital, chips, power, data and expertise, which creates barriers to entry and can concentrate power at the level of chips, cloud hosting, models and apps. A firm that builds its workflows on one provider may become locked in, facing price changes or service changes it cannot control. The course’s test question is whether you “can switch provider, export your data, or run an alternative model”; open-weight and smaller models broaden access, but they do not remove infrastructure costs or dependencies.

In plain words: only a few firms can afford to build top models. If your company relies on one of them, you can get stuck with its prices and changes. The test is whether you could switch, take your data with you, or run another model.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Listing issues one line each with no explanation. “Discuss” means explain.
  • Saying only training costs energy. Using the model costs energy too.
  • Writing vague issues (“AI is dangerous”) with no why.
  • Stating copyright is on the 2026 slides. It is not; it was accepted in the 2025 marking guide.
  • Confusing hallucination (invented content) with bias (a slant that repeats).

Key terms to use

  • training and inference: building the model, and every use
  • carbon footprint: climate gas caused by the power used
  • per successfully completed task: cost per job done right
  • hallucination: confident, made-up content
  • bias: a systematic slant in the answers
  • prompt injection: hidden instructions that hijack an assistant
  • barriers to entry: why newcomers cannot easily compete
  • concentration: power held by a few firms
  • vendor lock-in: stuck with one supplier, hard to leave
  • open weights: downloadable model, not its training data
  • GDPR / EU AI Act: EU laws on personal data and AI
  • copyright: authors’ right over copies of their work

True/False traps

  • “Only training, not inference, consumes energy.” → False. Why: using the model (inference) uses electricity too.
  • “Only the training of an LLM has an environmental cost; using it afterwards is free.” → False. Why: every answer uses electricity; millions of questions add up.
  • “LLMs can invent references even when they sound confident.” → True. Why: they predict text that sounds right, not checked facts.
  • “Prompt injection means malicious instructions hidden in a document or website that try to redirect a connected assistant.” → True. Why: that is the course’s definition.
  • “Open weights always include the training data.” → False. Why: you get the model’s learned numbers, not necessarily what it learned from.
  • “Downloading open weights gives you the model’s full training data.” → False. Why: like getting a baked cake without the recipe or shopping list.
  • “GDPR applies to personal data in prompts.” → True. Why: the EU’s privacy law covers personal data in what you type and what comes back.
  • “The LLM market is a single monopoly.” → False. Why: power is concentrated at several levels (chips, cloud, models, distribution), but there is not one single monopoly.

Block 1: True/False

On the exam: 20 points, 10 statements × 2 points. The paper says: “Read the statements below. Answer only whether they are true or false.” Write one word. Don’t explain and don’t hedge.

The pattern. In 2025 every TRUE statement was a plain fact from the course. Every FALSE statement had one twist:

  • a method used for the wrong job;
  • a weakness sold as a strength;
  • a benefit flipped into a cost;
  • a wrong definition;
  • a near-miss word (“unsupervised” where the course says “self-supervised”).

Read each statement, find its one claim, and hunt for the twist. Each “why” below explains its own words, so you can read this section on its own.

The ten 2025 statements

  1. (a) “In the Transformer’s self-attention mechanism, you can have two types of attention; causal- and bidirectional-attention.” → True. Why: self-attention is the step where each word looks at the other words to work out its meaning. It comes in two kinds: causal (look only at earlier words, used for writing text) and bidirectional (look both ways, used for understanding text).
  2. (b) “HuggingFace is a open source hub for Natural Language Processing.” → True. Why: Hugging Face is a free website where people share language models; the course outline calls it “the AI community’s open-source hub”. The grammar slip “a open” doesn’t matter.
  3. (c) “The input to a Large Language Model is actually not words, but tokens.” → True. Why: a large language model (LLM, the kind behind ChatGPT) reads tokens: chunks of text that are often whole words but can be word pieces (un | believ | able).
  4. (d) “To obtain word embeddings we can apply the Singular Value Decomposition to the PMI matrix.” → True. Why: the PMI table scores every word pair for “do these two appear together more often than chance?”. SVD squeezes that huge table into a few columns, and each word’s row becomes its embedding: its position on a meaning map.
  5. (e) “In text regressions usage of regularization techniques help overcome the curse of dimensionality.” → True. Why: a text regression predicts a number from word counts. With far more words than documents it can fit anything and learn nothing (the curse of dimensionality). A fine on big word weights (regularisation) stops that.
  6. (f) “Large Language Models are estimated using unsupervised learning.” → False. Why: the near-miss word. The course says self-supervised: the right answer (the real next word) is taken from the text itself. “Unsupervised” means there is no right answer at all.
  7. (g) “The Naive Bayes Classifier is great for capturing the relationship between two continuous variables.” → False. Why: the wrong job. Naive Bayes sorts texts into boxes (spam / not spam). How two numbers move together, like height and weight, is the job of regression.
  8. (h) “Dictionary-based methods allow you to capture polysemy in text well.” → False. Why: a weakness sold as a strength. Polysemy means one word with several meanings (“bank”). A word list counts “bank” the same way everywhere, and the course lists this as a problem.
  9. (i) “Multi-head attention mechanisms makes estimation of Large Language Models slower and less efficient” → False. Why: a benefit flipped into a cost. Multi-head means several attention “readers” side by side; they run at the same time (in parallel), and the course presents them as an improvement. “Estimation” means training.
  10. (j) “In the context of Large Language Models, zero-shot learning involves re-estimating only the final output layer of the model” → False. Why: a wrong definition. Retraining only the top layer is a kind of fine-tuning. Zero-shot changes nothing inside the model: you give an instruction with no examples.

Forty more likely statements

Turning text into numbers

  • “In a bag-of-words model the order of words matters” → False. Why: bag-of-words keeps only which words appear and how often; the order is thrown away.
  • “A DTM is typically dense” → False. Why: the document-term matrix (the table of word counts, one row per text, one column per word) is mostly zeros, which is called sparse. Dense would mean mostly filled.
  • “Removing stop words never changes the meaning of a sentence” → False. Why: stop-word lists (lists of very common words) contain “not”, so “not good” becomes “good”.
  • “N-grams can distinguish ‘low unemployment’ from ‘high unemployment’” → True. Why: an n-gram keeps neighbouring words together as one unit, so “low unemployment” stays one term.
  • “TF-IDF is a modeling step” → False. Why: tf-idf (word counts re-weighted so rare words count more) belongs to feature extraction, the “turn text into numbers” step, not to modelling.
  • “A term that occurs in all documents gets idf = 0.” → True (under the slide formula). Why: idf is the rarity weight. A word found in every document says nothing special about any of them, so its weight is zero.
  • “Rare terms get a low idf.” → False. Why: the other way round. Rare words get a high rarity weight, because few documents contain them.
  • “Euclidean distance is unaffected by document length.” → False. Why: Euclidean distance is the ruler gap between the tips of two texts drawn as arrows. A longer text has a longer arrow, so the gap grows.
  • “Cosine similarity of 0 means the documents are orthogonal.” → True. Why: cosine similarity looks at the angle between two arrows. 0 means a right angle (orthogonal): the texts share no words.

Methods built on word counts

  • “Dictionary methods are unsupervised” → False. Why: the course calls them supervised, because you fix the rule (the word list) in advance, so you know what comes out for any text.
  • “LDA is an unsupervised method” → True. Why: LDA, a topic model, finds themes with no answers given.
  • “LSA is a probabilistic generative model” → False. Why: LSA is table maths (SVD) with no story of chances for how words get written. LDA is the probability model.
  • “In LDA each document’s topic weights sum to one” → True. Why: each document’s topic mix is a set of shares, like 70% sport + 30% money.
  • “LDA always gives the same topics regardless of the random seed” → False. Why: the seed is the computer’s random starting point; a different start can give different topics.
  • “With λ = 0 the Lasso equals OLS” → True. Why: λ (lambda) is the strength of Lasso’s fine on big word weights. At zero there is no fine, so you are back to plain line-fitting regression (OLS).
  • “A high in-sample R² proves the text predicts the outcome” → False. Why: R² is a 0-to-1 score of how well a model fits. Measured on the same texts the model learned from (“in-sample”), it proves nothing.
  • “λ is chosen by cross-validation” → True. Why: cross-validation means trying several fine strengths and testing each on texts held back from training.
  • “Text regressions typically have n ≫ p” → False. Why: n is the number of documents and p the number of different words. It is the other way round: far fewer documents than words.

Next-word guessing and the creativity dial

  • “A bigram conditions on the previous two tokens.” → False. Why: a bigram model guesses the next piece of text from only the one piece before it.
  • “The n-gram model treats tokens as independent.” → False. Why: the whole point of an n-gram model is that it uses the piece before to guess the next one.
  • “Higher temperature makes output more predictable.” → False. Why: temperature is a creativity dial for picking the next word. Turning it up flattens the chances, so the output gets more random, not more predictable.
  • “At temperature 0 the model gives the same answer every time.” → True. Why: at 0 the model always takes its favourite word (greedy), so there is no randomness.
  • “Greedy decoding always picks the most likely word” → True. Why: that is the definition of greedy decoding.

LLMs: tokens, attention and input

  • “one token always equals one word” → False. Why: a common word can be one token, but “strawberry” is three: st | raw | berry.
  • “BPE merges the least frequent pair” → False. Why: BPE, the method that builds the token list, glues together the most frequent pair of neighbouring pieces.
  • “A Norwegian sentence typically costs fewer tokens than its English translation.” → False. Why: Norwegian words get chopped into more pieces, so Norwegian costs more.
  • “Masking hides earlier words” → False. Why: in causal attention, masking hides the future (later) words, so the model can’t peek at the word it must guess.
  • “Positional vectors are concatenated” → False. Why: the position numbers are added to each word’s numbers, not glued on the end.
  • “The original BERT can take inputs of 512 tokens” → True. Why: the slide gives 512 tokens as the original BERT’s limit.

LLMs: training and adapting

  • “An LLM updates its weights during each conversation” → False. Why: the weights (the numbers the model learned in training) stay fixed while you chat.
  • “RAG updates the model’s weights” → False. Why: RAG looks up documents and pastes them into what the model reads. It changes what the model reads, not what it has learned.
  • “Few-shot prompting changes the model’s parameters.” → False. Why: few-shot means a few worked examples inside the prompt (the text you give the model). The parameters (another word for weights) stay the same.
  • “Fine-tuning is a form of transfer learning.” → True. Why: fine-tuning reuses a model trained on one job for a new job, which is what transfer learning means.
  • “Fine-tuning a sentiment classifier needs labelled data” → True. Why: a model that tells positive from negative texts needs example texts already tagged positive or negative.
  • “Open weights always include the training data” → False. Why: open weights means you can download the model’s learned numbers, not the texts it learned from.
  • “Bigger is always better” → False. Why: results also depend on data quality, training and the task; smaller models can suit narrow tasks or running on your own machine.

ESG (environmental, social and governance) issues

  • “Only training, not inference, consumes energy” → False. Why: inference means every use of the finished model, and each use costs electricity too.
  • “GDPR applies to personal data in prompts” → True. Why: GDPR, the EU’s privacy law, covers personal data in what you type and in what comes back.

Business cases

  • “An unsupervised topic model is the best choice when the firm already knows exactly which concept it wants to track.” → False. Why: if you know the concept and its words, a word list or a Boolean (AND / OR / NOT) search does the job directly. Topic models are for discovering themes you did not know about.
  • “If a survey contains star ratings next to the free text, these can be used to train or validate a text classifier.” → True. Why: the stars are ready-made right answers (labels), so you can teach a sorting model with them and check it against them.

Block 5: The reflection question

On the exam: 20 points in 2025, and the question that most separates a C from an A. More than half a page, at most one page.

The point of it

The examiner wants to see judgement. Can you pick course methods that fit this firm and this data, say what number each one gives the manager, and say where each one would go wrong here? No maths is needed. You need to know what each tool does, in one sentence, and when it fits.

What the marking guide says: “A good answer should mention methods that are well motivation[sic] and relevant for the problem, and also identify one or more pitfalls with the proposed method in the current context. Would then be great if the methods complement each other in that regard.” BI’s grade A asks for “excellent judgement and a high degree of independent thinking.”

Three words from that guide: a pitfall is a way a method can go wrong in this particular case. Methods complement each other when one covers the other’s weak spot. Motivation is your reason for choosing a method here.

The 6-step template, in plain words

Think of a doctor: they don’t prescribe every medicine in the pharmacy. They name the problem, pick two or three medicines that fit, and warn about side-effects. Write your answer the same way.

  1. Turn the goal into numbers. Say what management will get, per person or per text. Example: “a tone score per respondent; the share of answers about ESG”. One or two sentences.
  2. Describe the data, and make one cleaning choice. Say what text you have and anything special about it (languages, length, ready-made answers next to it). Then make one judgement call, for example: keep the word “not”, because standard lists of very common words remove it, and then “not good” turns into “good”.
  3. Method 1: a simple, see-through first method. A word list or a keyword search (or a similarity score). Say what it does, why it fits here, the number it gives, and where it goes wrong here.
  4. Method 2: a method that fixes Method 1’s main weakness. If Method 1 needs you to know the words in advance, add a topic model that finds themes by itself. If Method 1 can’t handle context (“not good”, sarcasm), add a language model that reads context. Same four points.
  5. Check the results. Use any texts that already come with the right answer (tick-box answers, star ratings, or a sample you labelled by hand) to test every method. Compare with a simple yardstick, and test on texts the method has never seen.
  6. Name the wider pitfalls. Are the people who answered typical? Which languages? Privacy rules if texts go to an outside firm? Cost? Can you explain the method to management?

How to choose, in three questions:

  • Do you already know what you’re looking for? Yes → word list or keyword search. No → topic model.
  • Do you have texts with answers attached? Yes → train a sorting model, and check every method against those answers.
  • Does context matter (negation, sarcasm, double meanings)? Yes → a language model, not word counting.

The 2025 case

The question (verbatim): “You have started in a new position as an analyst in the communication department of a big international firm. The firm’s management team wants to improve their quantitative knowledge of how customers perceive the firm along multiple dimensions, such as whether customers generally have positive or negative feelings about the firm and how the firm is perceived in relation to ESG issues. To obtain information about this type of questions a survey was sent out - and over 1000 replies were obtained. In addition to concrete questions related to the above, survey respondents were incentivised to write half-a-page about how they perceive the firm. You are given the task to analyse these written texts. Given what you have learned in this course, propose at least two methods and analytical approaches you can take to address the management team’s desire for more quantitative knowledge. Be very clear about: the motivation for using these approaches in the current context; the problem they address and solve; potential pitfalls. Your answer should likely be longer than half-a-page, but not longer than one page.”

Read the case like an analyst. What is special here:

  1. Two separate goals: the overall feeling (positive or negative), and the themes customers bring up, especially ESG (how the firm treats the planet, people, and how honestly it is run).
  2. About 1,000 half-page texts. Too many to read and score by hand, but a small amount for machine learning.
  3. The survey also has tick-box questions on the same things. These answers can act as labels (ready-made right answers) to train or check a text method. Most students miss this.
  4. “International firm” means several languages. “Incentivised” (rewarded for answering) means some answers may be filler or copied.
  5. Management wants numbers it can average, compare between groups and track over time.

Words in the model answer, in plain words:

  • Stop words / negation: very common words often deleted before counting; negation words like “not” flip the meaning.
  • Dictionary: your own word list (happy words, angry words) that you count. #pos − #neg = number of positive words minus number of negative words.
  • Boolean search, EPU index: a keyword rule with AND / OR / NOT; the EPU index is the course’s example (newspaper articles about economy AND policy AND uncertainty).
  • Normalised by text length: divided by how long the answer is, so long answers don’t score higher only for being long.
  • Polysemy / sarcasm: one word with several meanings (“green”); saying the opposite of what you mean.
  • Topic model, LDA, K, random seed: a method that sorts texts into themes by itself; LDA is the common one; K is the number of themes you choose; the seed is the random starting point, so results can shift between runs.
  • Document-term matrix, corpus: the table of word counts; the whole pile of texts.
  • Supervised / unsupervised: learns from examples with right answers / finds patterns with none.
  • Classifier, Naive Bayes, baseline: a program that puts texts into boxes; Naive Bayes is a simple spam-filter-style one; a baseline is the simple yardstick you try first.
  • Fine-tuned BERT-type model with a head: a ready-made language model trained a bit more on your examples, with a small extra part on top that gives the answer.
  • Prompting an LLM, zero-/few-shot, output format, temperature 0: giving ChatGPT-style models written instructions, with no examples or a few; telling them exactly how to reply; the setting that makes them always pick their most likely answer, so results are consistent.
  • Held-out respondents, accuracy: people kept aside and only used for testing; the share the method gets right.
  • Overfitting, regularisation, out-of-sample: memorising instead of learning; a fine that stops it; testing on texts the method never saw.
  • Hallucination, GDPR, local model: the model confidently making things up; the EU’s data-protection law [Not in course files — general knowledge: GDPR is the EU’s data-protection law]; a model run on the firm’s own computers.
  • Uncertainty: how sure you are about a number; report it as a range.

Model answer

Goal and data. Management wants numbers on (1) whether customers feel positively or negatively about the firm and (2) how the firm is perceived on ESG and other dimensions. We have about 1,000 half-page free-text answers plus each respondent’s closed survey answers. I would first clean the texts lightly, keeping negations such as “not”, because removing them as stop words can flip meaning (“not good” → “good”). I would then combine three complementary approaches.

1. Dictionary/Boolean scoring: a transparent first measure. For tone, count positive and negative words from a dictionary and compute \((\#pos-\#neg)\) per 100 words for each respondent. For ESG, build a Boolean search in the spirit of the EPU index, e.g. (climate OR emissions OR environment) for E, (employees OR community OR human rights) for S, (governance OR corruption OR transparency) for G, normalised by text length. Motivation: simple, cheap, fully transparent and easy to explain to management; it turns each answer into numbers that can be averaged by country, segment or customer type. Pitfalls: word lists are subjective and must be chosen in advance; they ignore context, so polysemy (“green” as colour vs. environment), negation (“not sustainable”) and sarcasm are misread; general dictionaries may misclassify business words; and an English list misses answers in other languages.

2. Topic model (LDA): discover the dimensions customers raise themselves. Management’s list of dimensions may be incomplete. LDA on the document-term matrix describes each answer as a mixture of \(K\) topics (e.g. price, service, product quality, environment, labour practices). This gives each respondent topic shares and shows how much of the discussion is about ESG and which ESG aspects. Motivation: unsupervised, so it does not require knowing what to look for, which complements the dictionary. Pitfalls: 1,000 short texts is a modest corpus; the number of topics is a judgement call; topics can change with the random seed, so stability must be checked across runs; and the topic labels are the analyst’s interpretation.

3. Supervised classification, validated against the closed survey answers. The closed questions give each respondent’s stated feelings and ESG view. These labels can train a classifier on the text: Naive Bayes on word counts as a baseline, or a fine-tuned BERT-type model with a sentiment/ESG head. Alternatively, prompt an LLM zero- or few-shot with clear instructions, an output format and temperature 0. In all cases measure accuracy against the labels on held-out respondents. Motivation: contextual models understand negation and context far better than word lists, and the labels let us validate every method, including the dictionary and the LLM, instead of trusting them blindly. Pitfalls: overfitting with many words and few respondents (use regularisation and out-of-sample evaluation); LLM output can be inconsistent or hallucinated; and sending customer texts to an external LLM provider raises privacy and GDPR issues, so a local model may be required.

General pitfalls. Respondents who answer surveys, especially paid ones, may not represent all customers. Incentives can produce short or copied answers, which should be screened. Any text measure should be reported with its uncertainty and checked by reading a sample of texts.

What the formulas mean: “\((\#pos-\#neg)\) per 100 words” is the number of positive words minus the number of negative words, for every 100 words the person wrote. “\(K\) topics” is the number of themes you ask the topic model to find.

In plain words: tidy the texts but keep “not”. Tool 1 counts happy, angry and ESG words: cheap and easy to explain, but it misses sarcasm, double meanings and other languages. Tool 2 lets the computer find the themes itself, in case customers care about things management never asked about. Tool 3 uses the tick-box answers as the right answers, to teach a model and to check Tools 1 and 2. Finally: the people who answered may not be typical customers, some answers may be filler, and you should read a sample yourself.

Why this scores an A:

  • At least two methods: three, each named precisely.
  • Motivation in this context: each method has a reason tied to the firm’s two goals and the 1,000 answers.
  • The problem each solves: a tone score per person; themes nobody asked about, plus ESG shares; context-aware sorting that can be checked.
  • Pitfalls in this context: several languages, a small pile of texts, results shifting between runs, privacy with an outside LLM, paid respondents.
  • Methods complement each other: one learns from answers, one finds themes alone; one is easy to explain, one understands context; the tick-box answers check them all.

A second practice case

Short answer (2–5 sentences): A retailer receives 50,000 product reviews per month with star ratings and wants to know why customers are unhappy. Name two complementary methods.

Model answer

First, use the star ratings as labels to train a supervised sentiment classifier. Naive Bayes on word counts is a transparent baseline; a fine-tuned BERT-type model with a classification head handles negation and context better. Evaluate both out of sample against the majority-class baseline. Second, run LDA on the low-rated reviews to discover the themes behind dissatisfaction (delivery, size, quality), giving topic shares that can be tracked monthly. The classifier measures how unhappy customers are, and the topic model explains why. Pitfalls: topics depend on K and the random seed, and ratings are noisy labels.

In plain words: use the stars to teach a model to tell happy from unhappy reviews, and test it on reviews it hasn’t seen. Then let a theme-finder show what the unhappy reviews complain about. A “majority-class baseline” means always guessing the most common answer; your model must beat it.

Common mistakes that lose marks

  • Listing methods (“I would use tf-idf, LDA, BERT…”) without saying what number each one gives management.
  • Generic pitfalls (“models can be wrong”) instead of ones tied to the case: several languages, a small pile of texts, “not good”, people who answered not being typical, privacy law.
  • Forgetting part of the question: at least two methods, the motivation and the problem and the pitfalls.
  • Proposing an LLM as magic: no check of its results, no mention of made-up answers or cost.
  • Ignoring labels that are already there (tick-box answers, star ratings). They make training and checking possible.
  • Writing two pages. The paper sets a one-page maximum, and the extra time costs you elsewhere.

Key terms to use

  • supervised / unsupervised / self-supervised: learns from answers / no answers / text supplies them
  • dictionary-based: count words from your own list
  • Boolean search: find texts with AND / OR / NOT
  • topic model (LDA): finds hidden themes by itself
  • number of topics K: how many themes you ask for
  • Naive Bayes: simple word-counting sorter that learns from answers
  • baseline: simple yardstick your method must beat
  • out-of-sample validation: test on texts the method never saw
  • regularisation: a fine that stops memorising
  • fine-tuning with a classification head: extra training with a new answer layer
  • prompt engineering (zero-/few-shot): instructions with no examples / a few
  • hallucination: confident-sounding but made-up answer

Answer recipes

The point of this section: the 2025 exam used a handful of question types again and again. Each type has a recipe that earns full marks. The “command words” in the question (like “describe”, “why”, “max two sentences”) tell you which recipe to use.

True/False (Block 1, 2 points each)

  • Command words: “Answer only whether they are true or false.”
  • Recipe: find the one claim in the sentence, then look for the twist that makes it false: a method used for the wrong job (Naive Bayes for numbers), a weakness sold as a strength (word lists “capture polysemy well”), a benefit flipped into a cost (multi-head attention “makes it slower”), a wrong definition, or a near-miss word (“unsupervised” for “self-supervised”).
  • Length: one word, True or False. No explanation, no hedging.

“What is…?” and “What is the underlying assumption?”

  • 2025 examples: the bag-of-words assumption and the document-term matrix; the word-embedding assumption and the PMI table; the one-hot vector.
  • Recipe: (1) the core idea in the course’s words; (2) what it looks like, in words (for example, “documents in rows, words in columns, counts in the cells”); (3) a one-line example; (4) one consequence (“so word order is lost”).
  • If the question has two parts, answer each part in its own sentences.
  • Length: 3–5 sentences.

“Describe… include the motivation” and “Why…?”

  • 2025 examples: three ways to adjust the word counts, and the motivation for each; why position information is added; what a token is and why we use them.
  • Recipe: for each item, say what it is AND why you’d use it, meaning the problem it solves. Give exactly the number of items asked for, or at most one more. The “why” is half the marks.
  • Length: 4–6 sentences.

“Use a concrete example discussed in this course”

  • 2025 example: Boolean search, with a course example.
  • Recipe: first describe the method in general, then name the course’s example and its details. For Boolean search that is the EPU index: its three word groups (economy, policy, uncertainty), dividing by the total number of articles, the 10 US newspapers. A made-up example loses marks here.
  • Length: 4–6 sentences.

“Describe in intuitive terms”

  • 2025 example: the motivation for self-attention.
  • Recipe: (1) an example sentence (“bank” next to “water”; “it” referring back to “the animal”); (2) the analogy: query, key and value, like searching a library or a database; (3) the steps in words; (4) why it helps: it links far-apart words, works on all words at once, and you can see which words mattered.
  • Length: 5–7 sentences.

Compare two things: “difference / similarity”, “biggest conceptual difference”

  • 2025 examples: LSA versus LDA; word2vec versus BERT; fine-tuning versus prompting.
  • Recipe: (1) one line on each method; (2) one sentence that states the contrast directly (“Fine-tuning changes the weights; prompting changes only the context”); (3) what they have in common; (4) when to use which.
  • If it says “max two sentences”, write two sentences, no more.
  • Length: 2–6 sentences.

Draw

  • 2025 example: two arrows with high cosine similarity and two with low.
  • Recipe: draw and label two axes; draw arrows starting from the corner where the axes meet; mark the angles; write “high” by the small angle and “low” by the big one; add one sentence saying that arrow length doesn’t matter, only direction.
  • Length: the drawing plus 2 lines.

“Discuss shortly two…” issues

  • 2025 example: two ESG issues of LLMs.
  • Recipe: for each issue: (1) name it and say whether it is environmental, social or governance; (2) why it happens; (3) one consequence or fix. Two well-explained issues beat five listed ones.
  • Length: 2 short paragraphs.

Night-before checklist

  • True/False: one word. Hunt the twist: wrong job, weakness sold as strength, benefit flipped into cost, wrong definition, near-miss word.
  • LLM pre-training is self-supervised (the text gives its own answers). Word lists count as supervised (you fix the rule). Topic models are unsupervised.
  • Bag-of-words: only which words and how often; order and context are lost. The word-count table: texts in rows, words in columns, mostly zeros.
  • Three ways to adjust counts, each with its reason: shares (so length doesn’t matter), yes/no (so repetition doesn’t matter), tf-idf (so common words fade and distinctive words stand out).
  • Boolean search: key words + AND / OR / NOT, count the matches, then divide by the total. Course example: the EPU index (economy AND policy AND uncertainty words).
  • Topic models: topics are groups of words that travel together; each text is a mix of topics. LSA gives free-floating scores; LDA gives shares. Write the difference and the similarity.
  • Text regression: far more words than texts, so add a fine on big word weights (Lasso), choose it by testing on held-back texts.
  • Word embeddings: “You shall know a word by the company it keeps”. The PMI table scores word pairs against chance; squeezing it (SVD) gives each word its map position.
  • word2vec gives a word one fixed position; BERT gives it a new one in each sentence. “Max two sentences” means two.
  • One-hot vector: all zeros and one 1; in word2vec’s first layer it is a selection vector that picks out the word’s column.
  • Cosine drawing: labelled axes, arrows from the corner, small angle = high, big angle = low, length doesn’t matter.
  • Position information is added to each word, because self-attention sees all words at once and can’t see their order.
  • Self-attention: query, key, value, like a database search; each word mixes in the words that matter for it.
  • Fine-tuning changes the weights; prompting changes the context. Zero-shot = no examples, nothing inside the model changes.
  • Temperature is a creativity dial: low = safe and repetitive, high = varied but error-prone, 0 = same answer every time. It never changes what the model learned.
  • Reflection: two methods that cover each other’s weak spots, the number each gives, a pitfall in this case, and a check against the answers you already have. At most one page.
